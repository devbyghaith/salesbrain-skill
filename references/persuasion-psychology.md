## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Assume the buyer sees the tactic: persuasion knowledge carries about half the explanatory power of persuasion, and personal selling is where detection hurts most (Eisend & Tarrahi 2022, 148 papers). [study]
- Lead complex B2B deals with argument quality, not emotion: strong arguments beat weak ones most when buyers process carefully, the multi-stakeholder high-ticket case (Carpenter 2015, k = 134) (complex B2B, committee). [study]
- Drop "frame it as a loss" as a default: no general loss-frame advantage across 165 effects (O'Keefe & Jensen 2006); loss aversion (λ ≈ 1.96, Brown 2024) is weaker in expert buyers (Mrkva 2020). [study]
- Treat scarcity as an intentions effect: δ = 0.31 on 193 purchase-intention effects, from mostly hypothetical experiments (Barton 2022, 131 studies, 416 effects); fake scarcity is detected as a tactic (Eisend & Tarrahi 2022). [study]
- Use social proof as a small lever, not a closer: norm appeals work but effects are small (Rhodes 2020, 110 articles); the towel study failed in Germany (Bohner & Schlüter 2014). [study; mixed replication]
- Plan on real-world defaults far below textbook: d = 0.68 in studies (Jachimowicz 2019), but at-scale nudges averaged +1.4 points against +8.7 in papers (DellaVigna & Linos 2022). [study]
- Give value up front, unconditionally: prepaid incentives raised survey response 19.1 points while rewards conditional on responding had no detectable effect (Church 1993, 38 studies). [study]
- Present consistency traps as weak evidence: foot-in-the-door failed a direct replication (Gamian-Wilk & Doliński 2019) and induced-compliance dissonance failed across 39 labs (Vaidis 2024, N = 4,898). [study; failed replication]
- Use door-in-the-face for a verbal yes only (r = .126 verbal, .052 behavioural, Feeley 2012); "but you are free" is null in low-bias studies (g = 0.11, Fillon 2023). [study]
- Treat pressure and visible self-interest as net costs: reactance is anger plus counterarguing (Rains 2013, K = 20), and an easy-to-see ulterior motive is used against the seller (Campbell & Kirmani 2000). [study]

## What survives: principle by principle

| Principle | Status | Safe use in selling | When it backfires |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | replicated (Many Labs 1, 2014; Petrowsky 2025, 90 studies) [study; replicated] | Open first with a reasoned number in simple, price-only deals; rules and effect sizes in `references/closing.md`, section First offers and anchors | Multi-issue deals, random-looking anchors, a buyer who knows the market better than you (see `references/closing.md`) |
| Framing, gain vs loss | decision framing replicated (Many Labs 1); message framing null (O'Keefe & Jensen 2006, 165 effects) [study] | Use the frame that matches the buyer's own words; make the risk specific to their situation | Loss framing on expert buyers (Mrkva 2020); loss framing that reads as pressure (Rains 2013) |
| Sunk cost | replicated (Many Labs 1) [study; replicated] | Recap the evaluation work the buyer has already done when asking for the next step | It also binds buyers to incumbents; arguing them out of it triggers reactance, so de-risk the switch instead |
| Scarcity | δ = 0.31 on 193 intention effects, mostly hypothetical experiments (Barton 2022, 416 effects) [study] | A true time or supply limit for a high-involvement offer (time-based δ = 0.63, supply 0.50); say its cause | Fake or ambiguous scarcity in person (Eisend & Tarrahi 2022); demand-based cues on high-involvement offers (δ = 0.08); null in German and Dutch samples |
| Social proof | mixed: small consistent effect (Rhodes 2020); towel replication failed (Bohner & Schlüter 2014) [study; mixed replication] | Peer-specific proof, late in the cycle, when the buyer is uncertain; give the details an involved buyer will check | Early-stage calls (Gong vendor stat, no sample stated: 47% lower close rate); audiences already behaving well; "guests in this room" wording reversed in replication |
| Reciprocity | replicated for unconditional up-front value (Church 1993; Edwards 2009, OR 1.61); door-in-the-face replicated for verbal compliance (Genschow 2021) [study; replicated] | Give a diagnostic, benchmark or draft with no strings; concede from a larger ask to get a verbal yes | Rewards conditional on a meeting (no detectable effect); a gift or flattery right before an ask reads as a tactic (Fransen 2015) |
| Commitment / consistency | failed: foot-in-the-door (Gamian-Wilk 2019); induced-compliance dissonance (Vaidis 2024) [study; failed replication] | Ask buyers to articulate their own reasons (self-generated arguments shifted attitudes, d = 0.26, Vaidis 2024); ask for a self-prediction of the next step, not an intention (d+ = 0.29 vs 0.12, Wilding 2016) | "Small-yes ladders" and "make them say it" closes; asking about a behaviour when the buyer's attitude is negative can reverse the effect (Wilding 2016) |
| Authority | mixed: main effects favour credible sources, interactions depend on conditions (Pornpitakpan 2004) [study] | Establish credibility early for buyers who are not processing carefully; a credible source helps arguments and stories alike (Orazi 2025) | Prestige cues without arguments to a buyer reading the merits (Carpenter 2015) |
| Liking / similarity | hypothetical-only: no meta-analysis; mimicry null in a preregistered test (Hale 2016); Guéguen touch and mimicry papers retracted or under concern [study; mixed replication] | Ask follow-up questions; question-asking raises liking (Huang 2017) | Mirroring body language, touch, or a compliment just before an ask |
| Defaults | replicated but heterogeneous: d = 0.68 with 10 nulls and 2 backfires (Jachimowicz 2019); +1.4 points at scale (DellaVigna & Linos 2022) [study; replicated] | Pre-select one recommended tier, framed as the standard arrangement and a trusted recommendation | Buyer distrusts the seller's intentions; buyer holds strong preferences; default misaligned with what most buyers want |
| Priming (flag, money, incidental) | failed: flag and money priming near zero across 36 samples (Many Labs 1); the original authors' own meta-analysis ≈ 0 (Carter 2020) [study; failed replication] | None as evidence; set the agenda so the decision factor is discussed first, without claiming a study | Citing the flag, polling-place, background or flower anecdotes to an informed buyer costs credibility; see `claims-audit.md` |

## Writing a proposal: defaults and a recommended tier

- Pre-select one tier and label it "recommended": defaults that signal the architect's endorsement were larger (b = 0.32, Jachimowicz 2019, 58 studies). [study]
- Describe the recommended tier as the standard arrangement, not a novel add-on: options labelled the status quo are chosen more (Moshinsky & Bar-Hillel 2010, cited in Jachimowicz 2019). [study]
- Earn the default first: defaults weaken when buyers distrust the architect's intentions (Tannenbaum 2017, cited in Jachimowicz 2019), so lead with the diagnosis that justifies it. [study]
- Default: three genuinely different tiers, middle recommended; exceptions for indecisive, fixed-scope and expert buyers are in `references/offer-positioning.md`, section Building a three-tier proposal. [study]
- Put one strong reason under the recommendation, not a stack; the averaging evidence (Weaver 2012) is in `references/objections.md`, section Rules the model usually gets wrong. [study]
- In a written proposal, open with a short scene of the buyer using the result, then the facts: a blended message beat pure argument in writing (Orazi 2025, n = 450, small effect). [study]
- Expect the pre-selection to lift uptake by points, not tens of points, outside the lab (DellaVigna & Linos 2022, 126 RCTs); track which tier buyers pick. [study]

## Adding urgency in a close

- Use a deadline only when it is real and you can name its cause (price change, capacity, onboarding slot); time limits are the strongest scarcity type for high-involvement offers (δ = 0.63, Barton 2022). [study]
- Mistake: "how many others want it" cues on a considered purchase. Do this instead: name a real supply or time limit; demand cues did nothing for high-involvement products (δ = 0.08, Barton 2022). [study]
- Mistake: a manufactured "only three slots left". Do this instead: state the real constraint or drop urgency; in person, a visible motive is used against the seller (Campbell & Kirmani 2000). [study]
- With a buyer who already wants to buy but hesitates, drop fear and urgency (the 84% rule); diagnosis and reply in `references/objections.md`, section Diagnose before you answer. [data]
- Replace forceful wording ("you must", "no other conclusion makes sense") with a suggestion and a reason; choice-restricting language triggers freedom threat and can entrench the buyer (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Ask for a self-prediction of the next step aloud, not an intention (d+ = 0.29 vs 0.12, Wilding 2016); phrasing and timing in `references/closing.md`, section Ending a discovery call. [study]
- Default: describe the next step plainly to lower perceived stakes. Exception: do not rely on "you are free to say no" wording as the lever; it is null in low-bias studies (Fillon 2023). [study]
- Expect scarcity to move stated intentions more than signatures: the outcomes were purchase intentions in mostly hypothetical settings (Barton 2022), so do not forecast close-rate gains from a deadline. [study]

## Using customer proof

- Hold customer proof until the buyer has named a need and is weighing options: sellers using social proof closed 22% less overall and 47% less on early-stage calls (Gong; vendor stat, no sample stated). [data]
- For proof in cold outreach, see `references/outreach.md`, section Writing a first-touch cold email; the same Gong finding applies there. [data]
- Use proof when the buyer is uncertain about the situation or their own view; social validation works most under uncertainty (Fransen 2015, citing Cialdini 2001). [study]
- Match the proof to the buyer's segment and role, but expect a small lift: norm appeals are consistently small (Rhodes 2020), and "same room" specificity reversed in replication (Bohner & Schlüter 2014). [study; mixed replication]
- Give the details an involved buyer will check (customer, scale, result, timeframe): audiences who care about the issue scrutinise norm specifics such as sample size (Bohner & Schlüter 2014). [study]
- Let the customer speak where you can (reference call, quote, recorded clip): commercial sources are trusted less than other customers (Batinic & Appel 2013, in Fransen 2015). [study]
- Quantify the problem first, then tell the customer story: statistics move beliefs more, stories move intention (Zebregs 2015). [study]
- Keep arguments strong inside the story: argument quality and source credibility help narrative and analytical messages about equally (Orazi 2025, 308 studies). [study]
- Mistake: placeholder proof ("a similar company saw 30%"). Do this instead: a named, permissioned case, or `[NEEDS INPUT]`; vague proof signals a tactic and raises deception concerns (Fransen 2015). [study]

## Talking to an expert buyer

- Show the method behind each claim (sample, baseline, timeframe): careful processors weigh argument quality, so an unsupported number costs more than it gains (Carpenter 2015) (complex B2B, committee). [study]
- Expect loss framing to move an experienced evaluator less than a novice stakeholder (Mrkva 2020, N = 17,720); frame as gains or losses in the buyer's own words. [study]
- On calls, lead with clear logical arguments and keep the story short: analytical messages persuade more in audio (r = 0.40) than in writing (0.27) (Orazi 2025). [study]
- Mistake: leading with prestige cues ("who else uses us", confident delivery). Do this instead: lead with the strongest argument; cues only win when processing is shallow (Carpenter 2015). [study]
- Write the case so the champion can repeat it upward: committee buyers need a make-or-save-money argument they can explain to their boss (complex B2B, committee). [practitioner]

## Candour instead of tactics

- Raise the obvious objection yourself and rebut it in the same breath (O'Keefe 1999); the pre-empting rules are in `references/objections.md`, section Pre-empting the objection you know is coming. [study]
- Say what the offer does not do, early: buyers over-research because they assume the rep shares only good news (JOLT, 2.5M conversations). [data]
- Make your own interest visible and aligned ("we only earn on renewal, so I will not oversell year one"): deception concerns arise when the push benefits the communicator (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Recommend one option to an indecisive buyer rather than offering a menu; the JOLT recommend-and-de-risk sequence is in `references/closing.md`, section Buyer says "I need to think it over". [data]
- Ask the buyer to put the case for change in their own words rather than telling them: generating arguments shifted attitudes (d = 0.26) while the "free choice" lever did nothing (Vaidis 2024). [study]
- Mistake: "let me convince you why…". Do this instead: present the evidence and let the buyer draw the conclusion; forewarning of persuasive intent raises counterarguing (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Make the risk specific to this buyer ("teams your size hit this at month four"): optimism bias lets buyers accept a general risk while denying it applies to them (Fransen 2015). [study]

## Do not cite: the named cases

Before any study or statistic, open `claims-audit.md`; use the qualitative version of a claim unless a corrected number appears there. The four cases below come up most often.

- Guéguen's field studies (flower shop, touch, mimicry, "but you are free"): ten retractions, eight expressions of concern (Retraction Watch 2025). If the user cites one, say so and offer a replicated alternative. [data]
- Ariely–Gino "sign at the top" honesty paper (PNAS 2012): retracted after fabricated field data (Data Colada 98); the authors' own five replications had already failed (Kristal 2020). Treat as retracted. [data]
- Kahneman's priming chapter (Thinking, Fast and Slow, ch. 4; money, eyes, facial feedback): R-index 14 (Schimmack 2017); Kahneman conceded he "placed too much faith in underpowered studies". Quote the concession, not the studies. [data]
- "Croc brain" and the triune brain (Pitch Anything): the layered reptile-brain model "has long been discredited among neurobiologists" (Cesario 2020). Use "attention is limited" without the neuroscience. [study]

## Worked examples

**Proposal to a CFO-led committee.** The user asks for a three-tier proposal that "makes the loss of not acting vivid". Agent output: tier two is pre-selected and labelled "Recommended: covers the three systems you named in discovery". One reason sits under it: "Your team logged 14 hours a week on reconciliation; this tier automates the two steps that cause it." The obvious objection is raised and answered: "Migration takes six weeks; we schedule it outside quarter-close." No loss framing, no urgency line. Self-report: evidence level study for defaults and argument quality; the 14 hours is the buyer's own figure; the committee's loss aversion was not assessed.

**Buyer asks for "a few more days" after agreeing on the need.** The user wants "a deadline to force the decision". Agent output: "I won't invent a deadline. The real constraint is onboarding capacity: the next slot after the 15th is March. Given your audit, I recommend the standard tier. Will you bring this to Priya Tuesday, with my one-page case?" Why: this buyer already wants to buy, so fear and urgency raise indecision (JOLT); the constraint is real and its cause named (Barton 2022); the recommendation shares the decision burden; the self-prediction question is asked live (Wilding 2016).

## Common mistakes and replacements

| Mistake | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| "People buy on emotion, not facts" as a rule for every deal | Lead high-stakes, multi-stakeholder deals with a strong business case; emotion pairs with logic, it does not replace it (Carpenter 2015) [study] |
| "Losses loom twice as large, so frame everything as a loss" | Use the frame that fits the buyer's words; message framing shows no general loss advantage (O'Keefe & Jensen 2006) [study] |
| Manufactured scarcity ("only three slots") | A real, caused constraint or no urgency line; tactics are detected most in personal selling (Eisend & Tarrahi 2022) [study] |
| Consistency ladders ("get three small yeses first") | Ask the buyer to state their own reasons for change (Vaidis 2024); ask for a self-prediction of the next step (Wilding 2016) [study] |
| Customer logos and case studies in the first conversation | Hold proof until the need is named and options are being weighed (Gong vendor stat, no sample stated: 47% lower close rate on early-stage calls) [data] |
| "You're free to say no" as a closing script | A plain request with a reason; the wording is null in low-bias studies (Fillon 2023) [study] |
| Mirroring posture, touch or a compliment before the ask | Ask follow-up questions; mimicry showed no effect on trust in a preregistered test (Hale 2016), question-asking raises liking (Huang 2017) [study] |
