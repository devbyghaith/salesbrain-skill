## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Build three genuinely different tiers and recommend the middle; middle options won reliably across 142 observations (Neumann 2016), while decoys worked in only 11 of 91 attempts (Yang & Lynn 2014). [study]
- Use a decoy only in numeric side-by-side comparisons: a diamond retailer earned 21.4% of profit from decoys (Wu & Cosguner 2020); verbal or pictured options sent the effect to chance (Yang & Lynn). [study]
- Cut options only when they are complex and the buyer's preferences are unclear: a larger assortment averaged d = 0.02 across 50 experiments (Scheibehenne 2010); overload needs complexity or uncertainty (Chernev 2015). [study]
- Price on the buyer's economic value, not cost or competitors: over 80% of firms still price on those, and value assessment and value communication by sales are two of five barriers (Hinterhuber 2008). [study]
- Position as best for a subsegment of a known category rather than inventing one: almost half of pioneers failed (Golder & Tellis 1993, ~500 brands) and category straddlers trade at a discount (Zuckerman 1999). [study]
- Offer a flat rate or subscription before metered pricing: buyers who overpay on flat rates did not churn more, while pay-per-use bias raised churn (Lambrecht & Skiera 2006, consumer data). [study]
- Pair a prepay discount with onboarding or usage commitments: a prepayment's pull on usage fades over time (Gourville & Soman 1998), so cash up front can cost usage and renewal later. [study]
- Lead the offer with the few capabilities tied to the buyer's stated need: before use, buyers overweight capability, pick overly complex products and are less satisfied after use (Thompson, Hamilton & Rust 2005). [study]

## Buyer asks "aren't you just another X?": positioning steps

Dunford's components, run as steps in this order. The category comes last; the usual error is starting there.

| Step | Question to answer | Mistake the model makes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Competitive alternatives | "If we did not exist, what would the customer do?" (a spreadsheet, an intern, doing nothing count) | Listing only named competitors |
| 2. Unique attributes | What do we have that those alternatives lack? | Listing every feature |
| 3. Value | Why do those attributes matter to the buyer's business, in money or risk? | Stopping at the attribute |
| 4. Best-fit customers | Who cares a lot about that value, described by observable traits? | "Any mid-market company" |
| 5. Market category | Which frame makes steps 1–4 obvious to that buyer at once? | Starting here |

- Judge positioning by how fast a new prospect says "I get it, I get why you're different, I get why that's good"; the "grandmother test" is the wrong audience for B2B. [practitioner]
- Treat positioning as a cross-functional decision (product, sales, success, CEO): repositioning changes what sales pitches and what product must make true, not the homepage words. [practitioner]
- Draw the circle small: dominate a narrow segment with a capability the leader cannot copy, expand to adjacent segments, then challenge the leader in the broad category. [practitioner]
- Default: name the category buyers already use and claim a subsegment ("best X for Y"). Exception: when buyers cannot name the problem and nothing to compare exists, name the category and teach it; rare. [practitioner]
- Pass the differentiation test before pitching: what does only you do that rivals cannot match, and what would have to be true for the buyer to pay a premium for it? [practitioner]
- Say "different for buyers like you", not "better than X": procurement hears "we're better than X" as noise and routes you to its preferred-vendor list. [practitioner]
- Chain the early target market from differentiation: differentiation, the pains it removes, use cases, the persona who owns them, then industries; sell only there until it is won. [practitioner]
- Pick one frame when the product fits two: category spanning is penalized on both sides, audiences are confused and producers target worse (Hsu, Hannan & Koçak 2009). [study]
- Skip the "category kings take 76% of market cap" figure: its method is unpublished and survivorship-prone, and pioneer data point the other way (see `claims-audit.md`). [opinion]

## Deal stalls at "no decision": the four forces

- Diagnose a stall with the four JTBD forces: push of the current situation, pull of the new outcome, anxiety about the new, habit of the present. [practitioner]
- Read a stall as anxiety plus habit outweighing push plus pull; add de-risking or remove switching effort before adding more push. [practitioner]
- Expect the stall: analysis of about 2.5 million recorded sales conversations found 40–60% of deals lost to no decision, driven more by indecision than status-quo preference (JOLT data). [data]
- Safety nets that lower anxiety (guarantee, exit clause, shorter term, phased start), matched to the buyer's fear: see `references/closing.md`, section Buyer says "I need to think it over".
- Define competition by demand, not category: ask what the buyer would hire instead, including a spreadsheet, a hire or waiting; that list is step 1 of positioning. [practitioner]
- Match the buyer's trade-offs ("choose what to suck at"); overshooting what they want invites discount demands because you deliver more than they value. [practitioner]
- Answer "have you done this for someone like me?" before it is asked: expertise is the top selection criterion for most clients and one of few things that unseats a liked incumbent. [practitioner]

## Pricing a proposal: value before cost

Economic value estimation, as steps:

1. Commodity value: what the buyer would pay for the next-best alternative, from market prices or the cost of the status quo.
2. Differentiation value: what your differences add or save (uptime, hours, revenue, risk), in the buyer's own numbers, on their income statement.
3. Economic value = 1 + 2. Performance-proportional pricing is the floor, economic value the ceiling; price to capture a share and leave the buyer most of the gain.

- Find where on the customer's income statement you cut cost or raise revenue; if nowhere, there is no value to price, whatever the product does. [practitioner]
- Compare against the status quo when no competitor exists; "do nothing" has a cost, and buyers pay from discretionary budgets once it is shown. [practitioner]
- Segment by value received, not by volume: a plant running near capacity gains far more from uptime than one idle a week a month; price each segment accordingly. [practitioner]
- Expect buyers to discount promised value because the promise may fail on them; a guarantee closes part of that gap, and conjoint testing shows how much. [practitioner]
- Skip elasticity estimates (they shift week to week); compute the volume change a price move needs to break even, then ask reps whether the market will respond that way. [practitioner]
- Quantify one or two resonating points of difference against the next-best alternative, documented with the buyer's own numbers, instead of listing every benefit. [practitioner]
- Add committee members' individual values (reduced anxiety, reputation, ease of doing business) to the functional case; they move B2B choice beyond price and features. [practitioner]
- Value-informed pricing had "an unambiguous positive impact" on price level and market performance across 144 firms, and bad pricing practice erased product advantage (Ingenbleek 2013). [study]

Willingness-to-pay conversation, held before the proposal is written, simplest first:

1. Relative value: "if [a product they know] is 100, where does this sit?"
2. Price ladder: acceptable, expensive, prohibitively expensive; look for the cliff between answers.
3. Purchase probability on a 1–5 scale at a stated price.
4. Trade-offs between realistic packages at realistic prices.

- Segment by need, value and willingness to pay, not by persona; then productize per segment instead of positioning one product to everyone. [practitioner]
- Find the few features that drive most willingness to pay and price those first; giving them away in a free tier leaves only low-value features to charge for. [practitioner]
- Use "charge about 10% of the problem" (or one tenth of proven savings) only as a sanity check on the value estimate; it is a practitioner heuristic with no outcome evidence. [practitioner]
- Research the buyer's current cost before the meeting (team size, hours on the problem) and arrive with the savings for that team, then price as a fraction of it. [practitioner]

## Buyer asks "how much?": presenting the price

- State the price per outcome or per value metric the buyer understands (per tracked user, per mile, per goal reached), not per hour or per API call. [practitioner]
- Itemize only typical, high-benefit components (onboarding, support), mainly when the price is high: partitioned prices beat all-in prices under those conditions across 149 observations (Abraham & Hamilton 2018). [study]
- Attach the reasoning to the number (the model, assumptions, what it replaces); an offer without its justification is easy to ignore. [practitioner]
- Who opens, how ambitiously, and how to anchor a multi-issue bundle: see `references/closing.md`, section First offers and anchors.
- Present two or three complete packages at once rather than one offer; it signals flexibility and shows where the buyer can trade. [practitioner]
- Discount requests, concessions and self-labels such as "fair price": see `references/closing.md`, section Concessions.

## Choosing subscription, retainer, usage or prepay

| Structure | Choose when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Flat subscription | Value is ongoing with episodic use; buyer wants a predictable bill; you want to stop selling hours | Flat-rate bias is common and persistent and did not raise churn (Lambrecht & Skiera 2006) [study] |
| Usage or metered | Buyer wants low-commitment entry; value and cost scale with use; a metric the buyer sees as fair exists | Pay-per-use bias raised churn (Lambrecht & Skiera 2006) [study]; needs a trackable value metric [practitioner] |
| Retainer (prepaid hours) | Buyer wants reserved capacity and you accept selling time | A retainer still bills hours; a subscription lets delivered value rise without hour counts [practitioner] |
| Prepay discount | You need cash or the buyer holds allocated annual budget | A prepayment's pull on usage fades (Gourville & Soman 1998) [study]; pair with onboarding and usage milestones |
| Payment plan converting to recurring | Small-ticket buyer who cannot prepay | Hormozi reports lower churn on plans than on monthly billing; unverified [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call) |

- Decide in this order: pay-as-you-go versus subscription versus freemium, then the price metric, then the structure (a seats-by-departments matrix, for example). [practitioner]
- Test the model with equal-cost options (a percentage fee, a smaller percentage plus a fixed amount, a flat amount); the buyer's pick reveals the preferred model. [practitioner]
- Time-box services in 90-day increments and rescope at each end; longer scopes lock both sides in as needs change. [practitioner]
- Say "prepayment discount", not "paid in full", and present the full price before the prepay option so the discount reads as a benefit you give. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)

## Building a three-tier proposal

- Default: three genuinely different tiers, middle recommended (Neumann 2016; Jachimowicz 2019). Exception: when scope is fixed (an RFP, a set budget), one recommendation plus one alternative; a lone option makes buyers defer (Mochon 2013). [study]
- Exception: when the buyer shows indecision after the proposal, narrow to one recommendation plus one alternative and say which you would pick; options help early, narrowing gets the decision (JOLT; Mochon 2013). [data]
- Exception: an expert buyer with clear preferences gets the fuller menu, a first-time buyer the shortlist; informed buyers did better with more options (Scheibehenne 2010). [study]
- Differ the tiers on several dimensions (scope, cadence, named senior lead, response time), including non-numeric ones: more trade-off dimensions and non-numeric attributes strengthen the middle pull; a bare price–quality trade-off weakens it (Neumann 2016). [study]
- Mark the middle "recommended" and say why in the buyer's words; defaults lifted uptake d = 0.68 across 58 datasets, 2 backfiring (Jachimowicz 2019), and +1.4 points at scale (DellaVigna & Linos 2022). [study]
- Keep one top tier, not several large options: when several extremes appear, extremes start to look typical and the middle loses its pull (Sharif, Webb & Bhatia 2025, 6 studies, N = 9,377). [study]
- Use the top tier to serve the bigger-problem segment and to hold "killer" features wanted badly by few; sell those as add-ons and keep them out of the middle. [practitioner]
- Build each tier from leaders (must-haves most buyers want) and fillers (cheap nice-to-haves that make the bundle feel fuller), with no killers inside. [practitioner]
- Default: let a buyer take the top tier when the value estimate supports it. Exception: when the buyer shows indecision, steer to the smaller tier; a bigger price adds approvers and outcome fear. [practitioner]
- Group options under labelled categories when the menu is long; grouping eased navigation in single studies, and heavy per-option detail, not option count, drove overload (Scheibehenne 2010). [study]
- Name tiers by outcome or service level ("swim, boat, flight, private jet" to the same destination), not by module list. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Co-author the scope of the recommended tier with the buyer so it reads as built for them; if they have no process for the problem yet, sell a service first. [practitioner]

## Small-ticket, single-call offers: Hormozi heuristics

- Run the value equation as a checklist: raise the dream outcome and its perceived likelihood, cut time to result and effort; each lever is a place for proof, speed or done-for-you work. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Tie the price to the goal and timeline the buyer stated, then guarantee the outcome, not the effort: work continues at no charge until the goal is reached. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Use only true scarcity (limited capacity, a real next opening): scarcity cues raised purchase intentions δ = 0.31 (Barton 2022, 193 intention effects), but 384 of 416 effects were hypothetical. [study]
- Match the cue: supply and time limits work for high-involvement or hedonic offers; demand cues ("most clients choose this") work for utilitarian ones, where supply limits did nothing (Barton 2022). [study]
- Before adding any urgency, check for indecision; the 84% rule and the diagnosis live in `references/objections.md`, section Diagnose before you answer.
- Hormozi reports higher show rates after cutting false-urgency copy from booking pages; treat fake countdowns as a cost, not a lever. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Add bonuses only from fillers (cheap to deliver, wanted by many); killers wanted badly by a few stay as paid add-ons. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Step down in order when the buyer cannot prepay: prepay discount, half down, third-party financing, payment plan, then a booked next visit, so "everyone buys something". [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Sell the result ("sleep at night", "the June launch") and keep the mechanism for the proposal; a function sells to nobody who has not yet felt the benefit. [practitioner]

## Scripts

Spoken scripts stay under 40 words.

- Positioning opener: "We see this market differently from our competitors, so we built the product differently. Here are the approaches out there, including ours, with their pluses and minuses. Which one looks closest to how you work?" [practitioner]
- Willingness-to-pay ladder: "Three quick numbers: at what price would this feel like a good deal? At what price would it feel expensive but still worth it? At what price would you stop considering it?" [practitioner]
- Personal recommendation: "If you were my brother, I'd take the middle option. It covers the two things you said matter most, and we can add the rest once the volume shows up." [practitioner]
- Steering down an indecisive buyer: "I don't think you need the top tier yet. Start with the middle, prove the result in one quarter, then expand. That keeps your approvals and your risk small." [practitioner]
- Discount request: "I can take 30% off if we take 30% of the scope out. Which parts would you rather drop?" [practitioner]
- Value justification: "Your next-best option costs you X a year. Ours adds about Y on top of that in saved hours and fewer stockouts, by your numbers. The price is a fraction of Y, so most of the gain stays with you." [practitioner]
- Outcome guarantee: "If you haven't reached the goal by the date we agree, we keep working at no charge until you have." [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Buyer wants the cheap version: "If a logo is all you want, Fiverr will do it cheaper. What I think works is a brand transition over six months; here is what that costs." [practitioner]

## Worked examples

All figures below are illustrative, not benchmarks.

**Three-tier services proposal.** A demand-generation agency proposes to a 40-person SaaS firm whose discovery call named two needs: pipeline from paid search and a board-ready monthly report. Core: paid search, monthly review, shared account manager. Recommended: paid search plus the board report, fortnightly review, named senior strategist, 48-hour response. Full: adds account-based outreach and a quarterly on-site. The tiers differ on scope, cadence, seniority and response time, not on price alone. Recommended is marked and justified in the buyer's own words; outreach, wanted by few, lives in Full as an add-on rather than padding the middle. Each tier shows one total with onboarding itemized beneath it, and the seller says which tier she would pick and why.

**Repositioning from all-in-one to a subsegment.** A startup pitched "the all-in-one operations platform for e-commerce" and kept hearing "aren't you just an inventory tool?". Rework: the alternatives are spreadsheets plus the ERP's inventory module; the unique attribute is returns-aware demand forecasting; the value is fewer stockouts and less dead stock; best-fit customers are direct-to-consumer apparel brands with a few hundred SKUs and heavy returns; the category is inventory planning for apparel, a subsegment of a category buyers already use. The first pitch slide now reads "inventory planning for apparel brands that live with returns", and the sales story opens by comparing the three approaches, including the ERP module's strengths.

**Value-based price justification.** The buyer's next-best alternative is a contractor at 60,000 a year who reconciles manually (commodity value). The tool closes the books four days faster and removes about 30 finance hours a month; at the buyer's own loaded cost that is roughly 45,000 a year on top (differentiation value). Economic value is about 105,000; performance-proportional pricing would stop near 60,000. The seller prices at 78,000 a year, presented as "about 6,500 a month against a 105,000-a-year problem", with the model and assumptions on one page and a first-year exit clause to offset the failure risk the buyer is pricing in.

## Common mistakes and replacements

| Mistake | Do this instead | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| A dummy decoy tier in a services proposal | Three real tiers, middle recommended; decoys worked in 11 of 91 attempts (Yang & Lynn 2014) | [study] |
| Cutting options by reflex ("the jam study") | Cut only when options are complex and preferences uncertain; the mean overload effect was near zero (Scheibehenne 2010) | [study] |
| "Quote high; if there's no gasp, go higher" | A justified opening price; see `references/closing.md`, First offers and anchors | [study] |
| Cost-plus or match-the-competitor pricing | Economic value estimation against the next-best alternative, then a willingness-to-pay conversation | [practitioner] |
| Claiming a new category ("aren't you just a CRM?") | Best for a subsegment of the category buyers already use | [study] |
| Prepay discount by default | Prepay only with onboarding and usage milestones attached | [study] |
| Pitching every feature so "they see the value" | The few capabilities tied to the explicit need; the rest in an appendix | [study] |
| Fake scarcity or manufactured urgency | True capacity limits only; for indecisive buyers see `references/objections.md`, Diagnose before you answer | [data] |
| "We're better than X" | "Different for buyers like you", with each approach's trade-offs shown | [practitioner] |
| Citing "category kings take 76%" | Pioneer and category-straddling evidence; the figure is unpublished | [opinion] |
