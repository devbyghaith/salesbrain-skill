## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Quantify the problem and its cost first, then tell the customer story: statistics moved beliefs and attitudes more, narratives moved intention more (Zebregs 2015); "facts lose two-thirds of the audience" has no basis. [study]
- Stop any uninterrupted pitch before 76 seconds: across 67,149 demos no closed-won demo had a monologue over 76 seconds, while losers ran to 106 (Gong 2017); talk ratio was "not actionable". [data]
- Size story effects as modest: narrative exposure moved beliefs, attitudes, intentions and behaviour at r ≈ .17–.23 (Braddock & Dillard 2016), mainly by lowering counterarguing (Ratcliff & Sun 2020). [study]
- Pair every story with a verifiable claim: transported listeners stop noticing weak arguments (Escalas 2007), so a story that carries a weak case is a deception, not a technique. [study]
- Replace "tonality is 90%" (a 1967 artefact, Lapakko 2015) with what holds: concrete language raised real purchases (Packard & Berger 2020) and vocal modulation persuaded even when noticed (Van Zant & Berger 2020). [study]
- Show the two or three capabilities tied to the stated need, not a tour: win rates fell from 26% to 5% as feature-dumping time rose from 0 to 500 seconds (Gong 2021, 8,382 deals). [data]
- Leave out "croc brain", "lizard brain" and any triune-brain claim: the model "has long been discredited among neurobiologists" (Cesario, Johnson & Eisthen 2020); keep frame control as rhetoric only. [study]
- Lead a high-stakes B2B pitch with the business case: argument quality matters most when people process carefully (Carpenter 2015), and strong arguments helped narrative and analytical messages equally (Orazi 2025). [study] (complex B2B, committee)
- On a call lead with the argument; in writing open with a scene then facts: analytical messages persuaded more in audio, narrative more in writing, and 72% of marketers expected the opposite (Orazi 2025). [study]

## Building a sales deck or first pitch: narrative structure

Raskin's strategic narrative, as the deck sequence. All five moves are practitioner structure with no outcome test.

| Move | What goes on the slide | Mistake the model makes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Name the shift | Old game to new game in three or four words ("transactions to subscriptions"); the world changed, not "you have a problem" | Opening with the company or product |
| 2. Winners and losers | Named companies already winning the new game, and what happens to those who ignore it | Abstract "market trends" |
| 3. Promised land | The buyer's rallying cry in a few words, phrased as a question ("what would it take to turn every customer into a subscriber?") | A product vision instead of the buyer's goal |
| 4. Obstacles, then features as gifts | The things that make the promised land hard; each capability shown as the thing that removes one obstacle | A feature list with no obstacle in front of it |
| 5. Evidence | Customer stories and numbers proving others reached the promised land | Logos without outcomes |

- Open on the market, not the product: "we see this market differently, so we built it differently", then lay out the approaches with pluses and minuses, including competitors', and ask where they fit. [practitioner]
- Put the competitor into the hero's journey: a customer-hero, vendor-guide story has no rival in it, so it never answers "why you over them". [practitioner]
- Keep the buyer as the hero who must change; the shift story has no suffering protagonist, and the seller is the mentor, not the lead. [practitioner]
- Make the product a prop that makes the story come true; it appears only after the obstacles, and feature requests that do not serve the story get declined. [practitioner]
- Use the shift structure for group B2B buyers of a changing product; buyers of spec-compared products want the comparison, not the movement. [practitioner]
- Test a draft narrative in a few live calls before rolling it out: ask "am I crazy, or are you seeing this too?" and watch whether buyers add their own examples. [practitioner]
- Contrast what is with what could be from the first minute, widen the gap, then alternate between them; close with the call to action and a picture of the world after adoption. [practitioner]
- Build one "star moment" the room will retell (a live first look, a before-and-after) rather than a steady stream of slides. [practitioner]
- Open with an empowerment promise ("by the end you will know X, Y and whether we fit"), not a joke, which falls flat before the room has tuned to your voice. [practitioner]
- Say the core idea three times in different words, since part of any audience is fogged out at any moment, and build a fence: state what the idea is not. [practitioner]
- Signpost ("the second obstacle is…") so listeners who drifted can rejoin at a seam; after asking the room a question, wait longer than feels comfortable (Winston reports about seven seconds). [practitioner]
- End on a "contributions" or "what you get" slide that stays up during questions; ending with "thank you" tells the room they stayed out of politeness. [practitioner]
- Before the deck ships, check it against SUCCESs; most decks fail Simple (one core idea) and Concrete (specifics, not abstractions). [practitioner]
- Aim surprise at a weakly held assumption; overturning one the audience holds strongly offends instead of intriguing (Davis 1971). [practitioner]
- Open with an unresolved puzzle and resolve it at the end; the need for closure holds attention through the middle. [practitioner]
- Deliver the insight as a zoomed-out dissection of their situation ("here is the pattern in companies like yours"), then the method, then the packaged solution with its price. [practitioner]
- One strongest reason, not a stack: see `references/objections.md`, section Rules the model usually gets wrong.
- Naming the obvious objection yourself, with its refutation attached, in the pitch and the proposal: see `references/objections.md`, section Pre-empting the objection you know is coming.
- Keep the ask separate from the story: tagging an explicit appeal onto a narrative restored counterarguing in media experiments (Fransen 2015 review). [study]
- Run the Challenger reframe in order: an insight the customer may not know, where things are heading, then a tight transition to the one thing only you do. [practitioner]

## Running a demo

- Do the last thing first: open on the end deliverable the buyer named in discovery (the forecast view, the closed month), and show how it is built only if asked. [practitioner]
- Put value on the situation slide before any screen: the buyer's situation, the specific capability they lack, the deliverable, and what it is worth to them. [practitioner]
- Open with under two minutes of context tied to the buyer's top priority; winning demos did, then spent the first ten minutes on context (Gong 2017, 67,149 demos). [data]
- Partition the demo by job title and finish each segment before moving on; a linear "spaghetti" demo that hops between roles loses everyone. [practitioner]
- Invite a reply every 60–75 seconds: winning demos had 21% more speaker switches per minute, and 36% more in the second half (Gong 2017). [data]
- When to raise price inside a call or demo (after value, around the three-quarter mark): see `references/objections.md`, section Handling price pushback and discount requests.
- Spend the last minutes on specific next steps; winning demos gave them 12.7% more time, about four minutes (Gong 2017). [data]
- Demo before an explicit need is named, and the new-category teaser exception: see `references/discovery.md`, section Demo request before discovery.
- Match pace to ticket: a low-cost tool is demoed fast and early; a six-figure deal slows down and gets the right people in the room first. [practitioner]
- Let the buyer drive when possible and emphasize ease of use: hands-on experience shifts preference toward easier products (Hamilton & Thompson 2007). [study]
- Emphasize ease of use over capability count: buyers overweight capability before use and prefer simpler products after it (Thompson, Hamilton & Rust 2005), so feature tours impress in the room and disappoint later. [study]
- Tell one discrete customer story inside the demo (a customer with the same problem, what changed), under about 60 seconds or split by a question, not a narrative wrapped around the whole demo. [practitioner]
- Retell what you saw at a customer last week with a little drama ("I was with a customer two days ago who…"), then return to the screen. [practitioner]
- Say aloud what colours and highlights mean, slow down for non-native listeners, and avoid idioms whose meaning flips between countries. [practitioner]
- Treat the Gong demo figures as correlational, 2017 software demos: engaged buyers may cause longer, more interactive demos (won demos ran 47 versus 36 minutes) rather than the reverse. [data]

## Delivering it: voice, words, slides and questions

Slides. Kosslyn's eight principles turned into the violations the model commits most:

- Reveal bullets one at a time, keep each to two lines and lists to four items; 96%, 91% and 91% of decks broke these (Kosslyn 2012, 140 decks). [study]
- Group content in hierarchies of at most four per level and label items directly, not through a key; working memory holds about four chunks (Kosslyn 2012). [study]
- Keep type at 20 point or larger and avoid runs of all-caps, italic or bold text; 81% of decks broke the latter (Kosslyn 2012). [study]
- Go larger, around 40 point, when projecting to a room; fewer words fit, which is the point. [practitioner]
- Make the title the takeaway and the most salient element; vary colour, bullet style or background only to signal new information, since audiences read every change as meaning (Kosslyn 2012). [study]
- Cut detail that does not serve the point: "main point obscured by irrelevant detail", reading slides aloud and overfull slides were the most annoying faults reported by 205 frequent viewers (Kosslyn 2012). [study]
- Fix slides against the principles rather than by asking the room: audiences notice flawed slides but cannot diagnose them, and the harm is inferred from psychology, not measured (Kosslyn 2012). [study]
- Keep slides as condiments (an image, a number, a label) and say the sentences yourself; speech and slide text compete for one language processor. [practitioner]
- Match the chart to the message: lines for trends, bars for specific values, flow charts for sequences; label any ambiguous picture (Kosslyn 2012). [study]
- Print the deck and lay it on a table: dense text and no white space means it is too heavy; one dense slide per talk is allowed, with time to read it. [practitioner]

Voice and words:

- Replace abstractions with the buyer's specifics ("the March close", "your two Sydney warehouses"): concrete language raised satisfaction, intent and actual purchases across 1,000+ interactions because customers infer they were heard (Packard & Berger 2020). [study]
- Vary volume and get a little louder on the point: listeners read modulation as confidence without doubting sincerity, even when they detect it (Van Zant & Berger 2020, four experiments plus replication). [study]
- Use definite language ("this will", "clearly") in a big pitch, and name what must be true for it to hold instead of hedging the claim itself. [practitioner]
- Own uncertainty in first person ("it seems to me", "I think this works if…") and name the roadblocks; impersonal hedges ("it might") read as weakness. [practitioner]
- Keep certainty for the pitch and for seniority; in a small team meeting or as a junior newcomer, hedging reads as openness and overconfidence backfires. [practitioner]
- Pause briefly at the end of clauses (0.3–1.2 seconds) so the listener can say "yeah"; frequent pausers were rated more helpful in 200 service calls and two experiments (Van Zant 2025, small effect). [study]
- Keep pauses under three seconds and only where the other side can interject; longer silences lowered ratings and the benefit vanished when the partner was muted (Van Zant 2025). [study]
- Keep a composed, slight smile in high-stakes pitches: in a high-risk framing a broad smile cut perceived competence and purchase intention (Wang et al. 2017, lab study; inference for sales). [study]
- Keep a small-ticket pitch under three minutes: three pillars the buyer is missing, each with a 30-second story, describing what they will experience, not how it works. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Say only three kinds of thing on a sales call: questions, restatements of their words, and short stories that break a belief. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)
- Use a structure (problem, solution, benefit; what, so what, now what) so listeners can follow and recall; the "40% better processing" figure has no source, so claim the direction only. [practitioner]

Questions:

- Answer questions with what, so what, now what, and planned objections with problem, solution, benefit; prepare themes with evidence, not memorized wording. [practitioner]
- Handle a hostile question in three moves: acknowledge the concern (labelling rules in `references/objections.md`, section Handling price pushback and discount requests), paraphrase or reframe the question, answer with a prepared theme. [practitioner]
- Require an action from a remote audience on a fixed cadence (a poll, an "imagine…" prompt, a shared doc) and vary the format; Abrahams reports every 10–15 minutes. [practitioner]

## Writing the proposal narrative

- Mirror the pitch order: the quantified problem and its cost in the buyer's words, the shift or insight, the recommended option, how each obstacle is removed, evidence, price, next step. [practitioner]
- Put the recommendation and its number on page one (bottom line up front); readers go to the summary and the price before anything else. [practitioner]
- Default: the business case leads; committee readers process carefully (Carpenter 2015). Exception: a two-sentence scene may open the written piece before the facts; in writing that blend beat pure analysis (Orazi 2025, small effect). [study]
- Summarize current state, desired state and obstacle in their words from discovery, then ask "did I miss anything?" before the proposal goes out. [practitioner]
- Show the alternatives considered, including doing nothing, with the trade-offs of each; a buyer who has never bought this before decides more confidently with the map in view. [practitioner]
- Attach the model and assumptions behind the price on one page; a number without its story is easy to ignore. [practitioner]
- Make section headings takeaways ("payback inside two quarters"), not labels ("ROI"). [practitioner]
- Promise the proposal by end of day and follow up the next day; a proposal that lands a week later restarts the decision. [practitioner]
- The cover note's next action and date: see `references/closing.md`, section Proposal sent, now what.
- Send a short video between calls that covers social proof and the common concerns, so the next call resolves concerns instead of repeating the pitch. [practitioner] (small-ticket, single-call)

## Scripts

Spoken scripts stay under 40 words; the written one under 75.

- Empowerment promise: "By the end of this call you'll know what switching would cost, what it would save by your numbers, and whether we're a fit. If we're not, I'll tell you." [practitioner]
- Demo opening, last thing first: "You said Monday's forecast review eats half a day. This is the view your team would open Monday morning. Is this the number you need, before I show how it gets here?" [practitioner]
- Monologue break: "Let me stop there. Does that match what you're seeing on your side?" [practitioner]
- Narrative test: "Am I crazy, or are you seeing this shift too?" [practitioner]
- Confident uncertainty: "This will work for you, provided three things happen: your data sits in one place by March, one owner signs off, and we pilot with a single team first." [practitioner]
- Hostile question: "I can hear this one has burned you before. If the question is whether this stalls like the last rollout, here is what is different this time." [practitioner]
- Proposal summary, written: "Problem: your finance team spends about 30 hours a month reconciling three systems, and the close lands four days late. Cost: about 45,000 a year in time, plus late reporting. Recommendation: the Core plan with onboarding in six weeks. Price: 6,500 a month. Next step: a 45-minute working session with the controller on the 14th." [practitioner]

## Worked examples

All figures below are illustrative, not benchmarks.

**A problem-first pitch to a VP Finance, in two segments.** "Three numbers from your team: a nine-day close, about thirty hours a month reconciling, and two of the last four board packs late. At your loaded cost, roughly 45,000 a year. Does the nine-day figure match what you see?" The buyer answers. "A controller at a similar-sized distributor had the same nine-day close. Six weeks on our tool, she closed in five days the next quarter, same team. Her words: 'I got my first week back'. What would four days buy you?" Numbers move belief, the story moves intention, and each segment stays under 40 words, well inside the 76-second cap.

**A demo opening.** Situation slide, under two minutes: "Head of sales, 40 reps, forecast built in spreadsheets every Monday, missed two quarters running; what you asked for is a forecast you can defend to the board." First screen: the finished forecast view with their team names, not a login or setup. "This is Monday's view. The red column is deals with no activity in ten days, which is where last quarter's miss came from." Stop for the question. Only if asked: how deals get in. Then one segment for the frontline managers, finished before touching rep workflow. Price after the sales leader's segment; the final five minutes on next steps.

**A customer story used after the numbers.** After the cost is on the table: "Last month I sat with the operations lead at a three-warehouse apparel brand. In March they held 11,000 unsold jackets while the popular one sold out, because their forecast ignored returns, which run at a fifth of sales. Sound familiar?" The buyer answers. "We switched the forecast to count returns; by the June buy their dead stock was down about a third, and the planner said it was the first buy she didn't dread. Your returns run similar; here is your SKU estimate." Character, timeline, cause, one twist, verifiable on a reference call; the claim it supports stands alone.

## Common mistakes and replacements

| Mistake | Do this instead | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Story first, numbers later ("facts lose two-thirds") | Quantify the problem and its cost, then the customer story | [study] Zebregs 2015 |
| A three-minute monologue "to set context" | Context in under two minutes, broken by a check-in; no uninterrupted stretch over 76 seconds | [data] Gong 2017, 67,149 demos |
| A feature tour "so they see everything" | Last thing first; two or three capabilities tied to the stated need | [data] Gong 2021; [study] Thompson 2005 |
| Opening with the company and the product | Name the shift and the market; the product arrives after the obstacles | [practitioner] |
| Reading slides with six dense bullets | Takeaway titles, at most four items, revealed in turn | [study] Kosslyn 2012 |
| Hedged, abstract language ("we might be able to improve your processes") | Concrete specifics and definite claims; uncertainty owned in first person | [study] Packard & Berger 2020 |
| Using a story to carry a weak case | Pair every story with a verifiable claim | [study] Escalas 2007 |
| Citing "croc brain" or "93% nonverbal" | Cite vocal confidence and concreteness; drop the neuroscience and the split | [study] Cesario 2020; Lapakko 2015 |
| Ending on "thank you" or a "questions?" slide | End on the contributions or what-you-get slide and leave it up | [practitioner] |
