# Discovery and qualification

Open this file to prepare questions for a first call, run a discovery call, qualify a lead, diagnose needs, decide whether to pitch yet, or interview users for a new offer. Vendor call data (Gong, Huthwaite, JOLT) are correlational; bullets say so where it changes the advice. Output template: `assets/discovery-guide.md`. Numbers with sample and caveat: `references/benchmarks.md`; who said it: `references/sources.md`.

## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Choose question type over question count: in 35,000+ coded calls, explicit needs predicted success, implied needs did not, and open-versus-closed form was irrelevant (SPIN/Huthwaite). [data]
- Default: 11–14 questions spread through the call (Gong 2017, 519k calls); a 2025 sample put won deals at 15–16 (Gong 2025), so treat 11–16 as the band and type as what matters. [data]
- Treat talk ratio as a diagnostic, not a target: Gong's best ratio moved from 43:57 (2016, 25,537 calls) to 57% rep talk in won deals versus 62% in lost (2025). [data]
- Make most questions follow-ups to the last answer: follow-up questions raised liking through perceived responsiveness (Huang 2017; co-author Gino lost tenure in 2025 over other work, so flag when citing). [study]
- Spend more turns seeking information and checking understanding: skilled negotiators spent 21.3% of behaviour seeking information versus 9.6%, and tested understanding and summarized about twice as often (Rackham & Carlisle 1978, 103 sessions). [study]
- Hold the solution until the buyer states an explicit need in their own words; features offered before that create objections and price concerns (SPIN/Huthwaite, 35,000+ calls). [data]
- Put effort into responsiveness, not the opener: seller-initiated moves had little effect on rapport in recorded B2B first meetings; continuing the buyer's topic and echoing it did (Kaski 2018, 7 meetings). [study]

## Preparing for the call: what to look up first

- Look up company size, market and the likely trigger before the call; a buyer already researching suppliers finds basic situation questions redundant and may drift to another supplier (SPIN whitepaper p.18). [data]
- Research the individual, not only the firm: tenure, interaction style, expertise and interests, via the web and your network; sellers who did this found rapport openings faster (Kaski 2018). [study]
- For a group meeting, check names, titles, who attends, who is missing, and each person's likely agenda; missing this "silent information" damaged rapport in sellers' accounts (Jokiniemi 2023, 38 interviewees). [study]
- Write a one-line hypothesis of their problem in their words, plus what only they can tell you; without market knowledge you will not know what to ask next. [practitioner]
- Write down everything you wish you knew about each party, including why this deal now rather than six months ago, then add ten more questions. [practitioner]
- Budget about 10% of call length for prep on repeat calls (Hormozi reports six minutes for a 60-minute call); five minutes of research changes what you can ask. [practitioner]
- When research shows a certain poor fit, email to cancel and save their time; when merely doubtful, take the call and open with your reservations, since a wrong rejection pushes them to a competitor. [practitioner]

## Choosing the question type: SPIN table and practitioner tools

Use SPIN as a selection rule, not a fixed sequence. In major sales, implication and need-payoff questions turn implied needs (a dissatisfaction) into explicit needs (a stated desire for a solution), and only explicit needs tracked with success (SPIN/Huthwaite, 35,000+ calls). [data]

| Type | Use it when | Example (one each) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation | Only for facts research could not answer; keep to 1–3; skilled sellers leave fact-finding quickly | "What are you using for this today, and how long has it been in place?" | [data] |
| Problem | After situation, through most of the call; dissatisfactions in their words | "Where does the current process break down most often?" | [data] |
| Implication | Once a problem is named; knock-on cost, delay, who else is affected; most tied to success in large sales | "When that happens, what does it cost you, and who else notices?" | [data] |
| Need-payoff | After implications are felt; makes the buyer state the value of solving it; ask last | "If that were fixed, what would it let you do that you can't now?" | [data] |

Practitioner tools that sit on top of SPIN:

- Organise every answer under current state, desired state and obstacle, even when the talk jumps; the obstacle is what you sell against, and a minimum effective dose on all three is enough. [practitioner]
- Readiness ruler (motivational interviewing): "On 0–10, how important is fixing this? Why not lower?" The buyer argues their own reasons; MI-consistent questioning raised information yield in field interviews (Alison 2013). [study]
- Expect the ruler to inform, not commit: a 39-lab replication found arguing a position shifted attitudes only slightly and "it's your choice" framing not at all (Vaidis 2024, N = 4,898). [study; replicated]
- Ask why now: "What happened that made today the day?" (jobs-to-be-done); then map push, pull, anxiety about the new and habit of the present. [practitioner]
- Default: once a problem or preference is named, ask "why does that matter?" (need-payoff). Exception: in interviews about past behaviour, repeated "why" reads as interrogation; use "tell me more, give me an example". [practitioner]
- Play back their words slightly wrong or over-simply ("sounds like your process is blissfully simple"); people correct you and reveal the real problem (Voss-style label). [practitioner]
- Ask a root-cause question before any solution: "why do you think that's happening?" The perceived root cause decides what gets bought; problem size only decides that something is bought. [practitioner]
- Ask for a self-prediction, not an intention, when deciding whether to proceed; self-prediction beat intention questions, d+ = 0.29 vs 0.12 (Wilding 2016; wording in `references/closing.md`, "Ending a discovery call"). [study]

## Qualifying a lead: which framework, and what the model gets wrong

- Treat every qualification framework as a practitioner checklist: BANT, MEDDIC, CHAMP and GPCT came from vendors' own sales teams and none has peer-reviewed outcome evidence; the MEDDICC vendor publishes adoption figures only. [practitioner]
- Fill MEDDICC across several touches and contacts, never as one interrogation: too many questions greatly harmed outcomes with C-suite buyers (Gong 2017). (complex B2B, committee) [data]

| Deal size and committee | Use | How to fill it |
|---|---|---|
| Small ticket, one buyer, one or two calls | BANT, or goals/challenges/timeline plus a budget test | Three or four questions inside the call [practitioner] |
| Mid-size, two or three stakeholders | Pain, metrics, decision process, champion (MEDDICC-lite) | Across two touches; confirm by email [practitioner] |
| Complex B2B, four or more stakeholders, procurement | MEDDICC or MEDDPICC as an opportunity checklist | Across several touches and contacts; score gaps, not answers [practitioner] |

Elements the model gets wrong:

- Identify pain in the buyer's words and above the noise: "we need better visibility" is a desired solution; keep asking what sits behind it until you hear a business consequence ("I can't scale"). [practitioner]
- Attach the pain to a job measure: ask which number its owner is judged on and where it stands; a pain without a measure is a cut finger, not a broken arm (MEDDICC three whys). [practitioner]
- Test the champion by what they do without you: they pull colleagues, a boss or users into the next call and give you access to the economic buyer; enthusiasm alone is not a champion. [practitioner]
- Reach the economic buyer before forecasting: the purchasing contact often cannot describe how the product is used or its value; ask to meet the people who feel the problem and get their recommendation. [practitioner]
- Separate decision process from paper process: ask how the last purchase like this was approved (who, steps, how long), then ask about legal, security and procurement separately; the second often adds weeks. [practitioner]
- Qualify why now, not only why buy: without a negative consequence of waiting, and a workaround ruled out, the deal is a continuation, not an opportunity (MEDDICC three whys). [practitioner]
- Qualify decision-making ability as well as fit: 87% of buyers showed moderate or high indecision across 2.5M recorded calls; "let's revisit next quarter" is avoidance, not procrastination (JOLT). [data]
- Decide to pitch only when both hold: no disqualifier, and a high-priority problem you can solve; a low-priority problem stalls, and a problem you cannot solve gets acted on without you. [practitioner]

## First call with a senior buyer

- Ask fewer, higher-value questions and get situation facts from research or other contacts first; question volume hurt most with C-suite buyers (Gong 2017). (complex B2B, committee) [data]
- Earn the right to ask on outbound: under 60 seconds on the challenges you solve, no customer figure (early proof marks lost deals), then "enough about us, what's going on in your world?" [practitioner]
- Acknowledge their seniority and professional competence early; buyers judged rapport higher when the seller treated them as the expert on their business (Jokiniemi 2023). [study]
- Show your own expertise rather than deferring to specialists; buyers who see you as an emcee grant you no right to guide the choice. [practitioner]
- Weight implication and why-now questions: executives decide against "do nothing", which is usually one of their options, so the need has to be made stronger than inaction (SPIN). [data]
- Close for an advance you can date (access to another decision maker, an agreed next step), not a continuation; in large sales a call ending in continuation is coded as unsuccessful (SPIN). [data]

## The buyer who answers in one word

- Diagnose first: curt but answering means move on to problem questions; hostile or guarded means repair the atmosphere, see `rapport.md`. [practitioner]
- Ask for a story instead of a fact: "tell me about the last time that happened" draws circumstances a yes or no cannot. [practitioner]
- By the third question in a row, ask permission to go deeper ("this will sound redundant, but may I go one level deeper?"); buyers exhale and play along. [practitioner]
- Offer a wrong or over-simple summary so they correct you; once a buyer says yes there is nothing more to say, a correction keeps them talking. [practitioner]
- Pause at the end of a clause and wait; end-of-clause pauses drew assents and raised perceived helpfulness, mid-clause pauses did not (Zant 2025, 200 service calls plus experiments). [study]
- Ask why they took the meeting: "Is there a business need you have to solve, or has something from our side put you off?" [practitioner]
- Read "airy" circling around the topic as rapport not yet sufficient; return to their topic before more direct questions (Jokiniemi 2023). [study]

## The buyer who already researched suppliers

- Skip situation questions they have answered elsewhere; redundant fact-finding reads as unprepared and may push them toward a rival (SPIN whitepaper p.18). [data]
- Ask what motivated the search and go back in time: when was the problem first defined, in which meeting, with what action item? [practitioner]
- Ask what they have tried or compared and what they don't love about it; the answer is your real competition and the start of your differentiation. [practitioner]
- Ask who wrote the decision criteria and whether a competitor shaped them; if so, you are column fodder until you reopen the criteria. [practitioner]
- Name your weaker side before they find it, refutation attached (see `references/objections.md`, "Pre-empting the objection you know is coming"); a buyer who accepts a weakness because A and B matter most can decide (JOLT). [practitioner]
- Treat "do nothing" as still on the table and ask what happens if they keep the status quo (SPIN). [data]

## User interviews for a new offer

Mom Test rules (practitioner, no outcome evidence), with the data that back the same instincts:

- Ask about past behaviour, not hypotheticals, and discount compliments and feature requests: "tell me about the last time…" rather than "would you use or pay for this?"; people predict badly and cannot name features. [practitioner]
- Keep the pitch out of the interview; the goal is information about their life and the problem area, and a finished-looking product lowers honesty. [practitioner]
- Ask "how do you deal with it today?" and "what, if anything, have you tried?"; no attempt means the problem is not burning, and the attempt reveals your real competition. [practitioner]
- Extract three numbers per interviewee: what the problem costs today, how often it occurs, and whose budget would pay; stack-rank prospects on them. [practitioner]
- Interview recent buyers and switchers rather than people who merely complain ("bitchin' ain't switchin'"); habitual users invent reasons. [practitioner]
- Default: 10–12 interviews per round, since causal patterns repeat around 7–8 (Moesta reports). Exception: when segments differ by size, discipline and persona, aim for about five per cell (Kazanjy reports). [practitioner]
- Keep first interviews short (Migicovsky reports 10–15 minutes gives the best information), record or bring a note-taker, and expect your questions to improve after the first handful. [practitioner]
- Default: test demand with a commitment (card in an upgrade flow, pilot budget), not a yes. Exception: after past-behaviour questions, "would you pay $X? why?" finds the price cliff; see `references/offer-positioning.md`, willingness-to-pay ladder. [practitioner]

## Founder-led discovery

- Open with honest vulnerability: "We're early stage and have a lot to learn; can we get your insight into how this problem shows up on your side?" Assume the buyer knows more than you. [practitioner]
- Show less product; say it is not fully built to get raw feedback, since showing too much hamstrings the intel. [practitioner]
- Read pull signals: is the problem growing, are they measuring or managing it today, have they tried headcount or a tool; maturity of the attempt reveals the gap. [practitioner]
- Treat the buyer pulling colleagues, a boss or users into the next call as the momentum signal; absence after two calls means requalify. [practitioner]
- Move through gates in order: know the problem, prove the solution against their KPIs, one pays, many pay; skipping the first is the most common startup failure. [practitioner]
- Keep an advisory panel of people excited about the problem separate from sales targets; sell to them later. [practitioner]
- Treat most late-funnel problems as qualification problems: wrong person, wrong message, wrong problem, or not different enough. [practitioner]

## Demo request before discovery

- Default: diagnose before showing, since features before an explicit need create objections (SPIN); the demo waits for the explicit need. [data]
- Exception: in a new category, give a four-to-five-minute teaser at the end of the call to show the art of the possible, then return to their problem. [practitioner]
- Trade the demo for three questions: "Happy to show it. So I show the right 20%, can I ask what you're measured on and what's breaking today?" [practitioner]
- Set the agenda as a split before questions: "first half I understand your world, second half I show whether we can help; fair?"; buyers who agree to it tolerate questions. [practitioner]
- When a VP wants peers in the first demo, demo to the VP alone first so they advocate in the room, rather than facing two evaluators. [practitioner]
- When a demo is unavoidable, do the last thing first (the outcome they came for), in chunks by job title, rather than the product's workflow. [practitioner]

## Question bank

Each question is at most 25 words. ★ marks problem and implication questions, the types Gong and SPIN data favour.

Why now and context
- "What's going on in your business that's driving you to prioritize this now?" ★ implication [practitioner]
- "When did you first define this as a problem, and what meeting or action item came out of it?" situation [practitioner]
- "What happened that made today the day to look at this, rather than six months ago?" why now [practitioner]
- "What were you hoping to get out of today, so I understand?" inbound opener [practitioner]

Problem
- "What's the hardest part about [doing the job] today?" ★ problem [practitioner]
- "Tell me about the last time that happened: who was involved and what did you do?" ★ problem [practitioner]
- "Where does the current process break down most often?" ★ problem [data]
- "What have you tried so far, how long did you run it, and how did it work?" ★ problem [practitioner]
- "What don't you love about what you've already tried?" ★ problem [practitioner]

Implication
- "When that happens, what does it cost you, and who else feels it?" ★ implication [data]
- "What happens if you aren't able to fix this this year?" ★ implication [practitioner]
- "Does that take the [team, line, system] out of action, and does it create a bottleneck elsewhere?" ★ implication [data]
- "Which number you're measured on does this show up in, and where is it today?" ★ implication [practitioner]
- "Why do you think that's happening?" ★ implication, root cause [practitioner]

Need-payoff and desired state
- "If that were fixed, what would it let you do that you can't do now?" need-payoff [data]
- "Why does [compact, fast, simple] matter to you? How does it help?" need-payoff [data]
- "On 0–10, how important is solving this? Why not lower?" readiness ruler [practitioner]
- "Three months in, what would tell you this was the right call?" success measure [practitioner]

Qualification and process
- "Who owns that number, and who signs if this costs more than what's budgeted?" economic buyer [practitioner]
- "Walk me through how the last purchase like this got approved: who, which steps, how long?" decision and paper process [practitioner]
- "What else could you do instead, including nothing?" competition and status quo [practitioner]
- "Can I check something? I'm sensing this isn't an intense enough challenge for me to help with. Am I right?" disqualify [practitioner]

Checking understanding
- "Let me make sure I understand… [one paragraph in their words]. Did I get that right?" every three or four questions [study]
- "Sounds like your process is blissfully simple and easy." wrong label that invites correction [practitioner]

## Worked examples

**Inbound "we need visibility" (mid-market SaaS)**
- Setup: a VP Sales books a call and says "we need better visibility into deals"; the usual move is to demo dashboards at that sentence. [practitioner]
- Moves: four follow-ups in a row, "what's behind that?", "what happens if you can't?", "why prioritize it now?", "what does that do to cash?" [practitioner]
- Result: visibility meant reps not selling to power, a nine-month cycle against a six-month plan, net-60 terms stretching cash, a bridge round at risk. [practitioner]
- Lesson: the explicit need and the willingness to pay surfaced at the fourth follow-up, not the first; the demo came after. [practitioner]

**First call with a COO, 25 minutes**
- Setup: situation facts (size, stack, recent hire) came from research; the plan is nine questions, not fourteen, because volume hurts with executives (Gong 2017). [data]
- Moves: under 60 seconds of point of view, no customer figure; two problem questions; three implication questions on cost and who else is hit; one need-payoff; one summary. [practitioner]
- Result: the COO states "we lose two weeks a quarter to rework"; the advance is a dated session with the CFO who owns that number; no product shown. [practitioner]

**User interview for a new analytics offer**
- Mistake: "Would you pay $200 a month for this?" gets a polite yes and a trial that never starts. [practitioner]
- Replacement: "Tell me about the last time you needed this number. What did you do? What did it cost you?" [practitioner]
- Result: they built a spreadsheet, spend three hours a week on it, and nobody owns a budget; find the budget owner or drop the segment. [practitioner]

## Common mistakes and replacements

- Counting questions or insisting on open-ended ones → choose the type: problem, then implication, then need-payoff; form did not predict success (SPIN). [data]
- Front-loading seven to twelve questions in a row → spread them through the call, summarize every three or four, and ask permission before going deeper (Gong 2017). [data]
- Pitching at the first stated "need" → treat it as a desired solution; ask what sits behind it and what happens if it stays unfixed. [practitioner]
- Chasing a 90/10 or 2:1 listening ratio → cut long seller monologues, which marked lost deals, and read the ratio as a symptom; consistency mattered more than the exact split (Gong 2025). [data]
- "What keeps you up at night?", "magic wand", "what features would you want?" → ask about the last specific episode and what they tried; the generic answers change daily. [practitioner]
- "Would you use or pay for this?" → ask for a commitment (card, pilot budget) or for past behaviour. [practitioner]
- Running MEDDICC as twenty questions in one call → fill it across touches and contacts; question volume harmed executive calls. [data]
- Situation questions a researched buyer has answered elsewhere → look up size and market first and ask only what research cannot answer (SPIN). [data]
- Leading questions that route back to what you sell ("you need a new site") → one neutral observation plus an open question; ignoring surprising answers kills rapport. [practitioner]
- Justifying "why not lower?" with consistency pressure → use it to surface the buyer's own reasons; consistency and free-choice effects failed large replications (Vaidis 2024). [study; replicated]
