# Objections, stalls and brush-offs

Open this file when a buyer pushes back, stalls or brushes you off. Diagnose first, then jump to the situation
header. Full source entries live in `benchmarks.md`; claims excluded as failed, retracted or unverifiable are
listed in `claims-audit.md`. Vendor figures (Gong, JOLT, Huthwaite) are correlational, not causal.

## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Diagnose indecision versus status-quo preference before responding: 40–60% of deals end in no decision, and just under half of those are status-quo preference (JOLT 2022, about 2.5M conversations). [data]
- Drop fear and urgency on an indecisive buyer: dialling up FOMO made indecisive buyers more indecisive 84% of the time (JOLT, 2.5M conversations). Recommend, narrow, de-risk instead. [data]
- Pause, then ask a clarifying question before answering: top reps pause longer after objections; average reps launch a 21-second monologue (Gong 2018, 67,149 demos; correlational). [data]
- Answer with your single strongest reason, not a stack: evaluators average, so mild extra points lower the judgement (Weaver, Garcia & Schwarz 2012, 7 experiments; skilled negotiators 1.8 reasons vs 3, Huthwaite). [study]
- Pre-empt an objection only with its refutation attached: refutational two-sided messages beat one-sided; naming a weakness without rebutting it persuades less (O'Keefe 1999 meta-analysis). [study]
- Treat objection volume as a discovery-quality signal: in complex sales top performers receive about a third as many objections, because pitching benefits before explicit needs creates them (SPIN, Huthwaite 35,000+ calls). [data]
- Classify the motive before choosing a response: threat to freedom wants autonomy, reluctance to change wants lower switching cost, suspicion of deception wants proof (Fransen, Smit & Verlegh 2015; links untested). [study]
- Raise price on the first call, late in that call: first-call price talk tracked a 42% win rate versus 5% when never discussed (Gong 2020, 11,331 opportunities; correlational). [data]

## Diagnose before you answer

Run these four questions before replying; each answer changes the move. Most stalls are lost here, not at the rebuttal.

| Question | What you hear | Then do this |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A reason with substance, or a reasonless line? | "I'm walking into a board meeting"; "we signed with X in March" | A truth, not an objection: acknowledge, ask one light question or none, come back on a named day. [practitioner] |
| | "send me info"; "next quarter"; "think about it"; "send a proposal" | Probably a brush-off: pause two beats, give control back, ask permission for one question. [practitioner] |
| 2. Indifferent or indecisive? | Indifferent: no pain named, "we're fine as we are" | Status-quo preference: build the cost of inaction and contrast futures; urgency belongs here only. [data] |
| | Indecisive: agreed on value, still researching, adding evaluators, asking "what if" | Fear of a mistake: recommend one option, limit exploration, take risk off the table; no fear appeals. [data] |
| 3. Which motive drives the resistance? | Freedom threat: bristles at closes, "don't push me" | Hand the decision back, use "it's your call" language, cut imperatives. [study] |
| | Reluctance to change: "it works well enough", habit, switching cost | Lower the cost and risk of change: migration help, shorter term, start small. [study] |
| | Deception concern: "what's the catch", asks for proof, probes your motive | Transparency, verifiable evidence, name your weakness first. [study] |
| 4. Which fear sits under a stall? | Wrong configuration or term; bad news after signing; ROI not realised | Match the safety net to that fear: trial, exit clause, phased start, implementation support. [data] |

- Judge indecision from behaviour, not words: a buyer who keeps adding evaluators, requesting references and asking "what if" after agreeing on value is indecisive, not unconvinced (JOLT, 2.5M conversations). [data]
- Surface the unspoken objection when the yes is soft ("I guess so"): say "I sense some hesitation, what's holding you back?"; top reps address every objection, average reps about 70% (JOLT). [data]
- Read a flat, silent reaction as ambiguous: outcome resistance (no change) and motivated resistance (defending the current view) look the same; ask before concluding the buyer is resisting (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Decide the motion before picking tactics: lines marked (small-ticket, single-call) below are exception paths for one-call consumer closes; in committee B2B they lower trust and deepen indecision (Hawes 1996; JOLT). [study]

If the user asks for a named framework, one selection line each:

| Framework | Use it for | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| JOLT (judge, offer, limit, take risk off) | Late-stage stalls from an indecisive buyer; the default in this file. | [data] |
| Voss labels and calibrated questions | Drawing out the real concern without a counter-argument; practitioner tool, no sales test. | [practitioner] |
| LAER (listen, acknowledge, explore, respond) | Only its explore step matters: ask before answering; no peer-reviewed test. | [practitioner] |
| Feel-Felt-Found | Skip in B2B: scripted empathy reads as a tactic and invites contesting; no test. | [practitioner] |
| CLOSER | (small-ticket, single-call) one-call script; its "explain away concerns" step is the pressure path. | [practitioner] |
| Sandler up-front contract | Agreeing agenda and next-step options at the top of a first call; vendor-endorsed, untested. | [practitioner] |

## Prospect says "it's too expensive"

- Default: ask what the price is being compared to before defending it; "expensive" against budget, against another quote, or against expected return each needs a different answer. [practitioner]
- Then answer with the one value reason tied to the explicit need they stated; four price angles and memorised stories dilute the strong reason (Weaver, Garcia & Schwarz 2012). [study]
- Exception: when a buyer positioned on premium early says "almost double what we pay now", treat it as a disguised discount request and hold price; go to the price section. [practitioner]
- Exception (small-ticket, single-call): "is this a lot of money to you? then you'll try harder" reframes belong to one-call consumer closes; in a committee deal, ask the comparison question instead. [practitioner]
- Script: "Help me understand the comparison: is it over the budget you set, above another quote, or hard to tie to the return we mapped? I'll answer the one that matters." [practitioner]
- Mistake: defending the price with a stack of justifications in a 21-second monologue → pause, one clarifying question, then one strong reason (Gong 2018, 67,149 demos). [data]

## Prospect says "I need to think about it"

- Default: treat it as indecision until proven otherwise; ask whether a question is still open or whether they are unsure how to decide, then recommend one option and remove risk (JOLT). [data]
- Listen for a maximiser ("best at everything") versus a satisficer ("best on two things, good enough elsewhere"); maximisers stall, so narrow their criteria before narrowing options (JOLT). [data]
- Make the recommendation personal and often smaller: "if this were my team, I'd start with B"; a bigger price widens the buying group and adds outcome fear (JOLT). [data]
- Limit exploration: say what to leave out of the proposal, cap added evaluators, and answer every open objection on the call rather than scheduling more research (JOLT). [data]
- Offer a safety net matched to the fear: trial, exit clause, phased start, implementation support (complex B2B); guarantees and prorated refunds (small-ticket); reset expectations away from the spectacular case study (JOLT). [data]
- Trade term for comfort: "take the three-year price, sign for one year", or start with one unit; a smaller first deal that works sells more over time (JOLT). [practitioner]
- Hand the decision back in plain words when the buyer bristles (reactance, Fransen 2015); do not count on "but you are free" as a lever, it was null in low-bias tests (Fillon 2023). [study]
- Exception: when no explicit need was ever named, this is status-quo preference, not indecision; return to problem and implication questions (`references/discovery.md`) rather than closing (SPIN). [data]
- Exception (small-ticket, single-call): past/present/future questioning, the rocking-chair story and "indecision is a decision" are one-call pressure lines; they deepen indecision in considered purchases. [practitioner]
- Script: "Happy to give you time. Is there a question we haven't answered, or is it more about being sure this is the right call? If the latter, here's what I'd recommend and why." [data]
- Script: "What's your main concern? Let's think it through together." works in any motion as the clarifying question. [practitioner]
- Mistake: benefits recap, then urgency, then a quarter-end discount (75% of reps' first move, JOLT) → "what would make you comfortable this is the right choice?", then one recommendation. [data]

## Cold prospect says "send me some info" or "not interested"

- Treat a reasonless cold-call brush-off as a reflex to the interruption, not a verdict on your offer; diagnosis and scripts live in `references/outreach.md`, section Prospect says "not interested" or "send me some info". [practitioner]

## Prospect says "we're happy with our current vendor"

- Default: treat "we have a vendor" as a truth, not an objection; respond with one specific problem question about a gap their vendor type usually leaves, not a feature pitch. [practitioner]
- If you hear it on most calls, your message is not meaningfully different from what they already have; fix the positioning and the problem question, not the rebuttal. [practitioner]
- Treat it as reluctance to change: lower the switching cost (migration, running both for a period, shorter first term) rather than attacking the incumbent (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Reduce friction instead of adding features: anxiety about the new and habit of the present hold buyers back, and more features add anxiety ("can it do all that?"). [practitioner]
- Exception: when the buyer names an explicit dissatisfaction with the incumbent, move straight to implication questions on that gap; switching-cost talk can wait (SPIN). [data]
- Script: "Makes sense, most teams we talk to have something in place. Out of curiosity, how are you handling [specific gap] with it today?" [practitioner]
- Mistake: listing why you beat the incumbent → one problem question, then silence; attacking a vendor they chose triggers biased processing in its favour (Fransen 2015). [study]

## Prospect says "there's no budget"

- Default: ask whether it is no budget line, budget committed elsewhere, or no case yet strong enough to move money; only the third is something this call can change. [practitioner]
- Ask about budget early rather than tiptoe: budget discussed on the first call tracked a 49% win rate versus 7% when never discussed (Gong 2020, 11,331 opportunities; correlational). [data]
- If the explicit need is weak, go back to problem and implication questions; budget appears when the cost of the problem is quantified, not when you discount (SPIN). [data]
- Exception: when a genuine freeze exists, propose a scoped pilot or a start date with paperwork done now; don't manufacture urgency against a freeze you cannot move. [practitioner]
- Exception (small-ticket, single-call): "people find money when someone else needs it" and resourcefulness stories are consumer-close pressure; with a procurement-governed buyer, scope a pilot to the line that exists instead. [practitioner]
- Script: "Understood. Is it that there's no line for this, that it's committed elsewhere, or that nobody has yet made a case strong enough to move money? The third one I can help with." [practitioner]
- Mistake: discounting to fit the stated budget → keep the price and change the scope (a de-featured version); see the price section below. [practitioner]

## Prospect says "it's not the right time"

- Default: ask what would be different at the better time; if nothing concrete changes ("after the quarter"), it is a stall, so handle it as indecision or a brush-off. [practitioner]
- When the trigger is real (migration, hiring freeze, audit), agree a named date and the first step on it; specific first-meeting next steps track with faster, won deals (Gong 2018, 28,833 deals). [data]
- Have the buyer state who does what by when, and what happens if it is blocked; if-then plans raise follow-through (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024, 642 tests). [study]
- Exception (small-ticket, single-call): "you'll be busy again, so start now while busy" is a one-call pressure line; in B2B, plan the implementation around the busy period instead. [practitioner]
- Script: "What changes on that date that makes it the better time? If it's [X], we can plan the start around it now and keep today's terms." [practitioner]
- Mistake: accepting "not now" and leaving with no date → a calendar entry, an owner on their side, and the agreed fallback if the date slips. [data]

## Prospect says "I need to run it by my boss" or a committee

- Default: ask what the boss will push on that you haven't covered, then arm the champion with a one-page answer to exactly that; the boss doesn't know you, the champion is your pitch. [practitioner]
- Ask "who else needs to be part of this decision?" before price, not after; then get them into the next meeting rather than selling through a relay. [practitioner]
- With a committee, indecision multiplies: each added evaluator adds fear of blame, so give the champion one recommendation and a comparison they can forward as a defensible choice (JOLT). [data]
- Exception: when "I'll have to ask my boss" comes from procurement during a price demand, it signals a discount process exists; don't escalate with a number, trade instead (price section). [practitioner]
- Exception (small-ticket, single-call): "support, not permission" and "if roles were reversed, would you back them?" are spouse-objection lines for consumer closes; with a manager, arm the champion instead. [practitioner]
- Script: "Makes sense. When you take it to [name], what's the first thing they'll push on? Let's build the answer together so you're not carrying it alone." [practitioner]
- Mistake: asking to present to the boss yourself as the first move → equip the champion first; offer to join only once you know the boss's actual question. [practitioner]

## Prospect says "your competitor is cheaper" or asks for a comparison

- Default: ask which capability the comparison is on; a lower price for a narrower scope is not a lower price. Compare on the one differentiated capability tied to their explicit need. [practitioner]
- Concede the competitor's genuine strengths before stating yours: "they're very good at X; where we differ is Y" is the refutational two-sided form that raises credibility (O'Keefe 1999). [study]
- When they compare you to a product you don't compete with ("you're like Salesforce") or ask "can't an intern do this?", it is a positioning failure; restate the alternative you actually replace. [practitioner]
- Exception: if you are usually the highest bid, raise it yourself first, tell them what to check in cheaper quotes and why, then "call me only if you're not fully confident". [practitioner]
- Script: "They're strong on X, genuinely. Two things to check in their quote: [A] and [B]. If both are covered at that price, take it. If not, that's the gap we're priced for." [practitioner]
- Mistake: matching the competitor's price to win → match scope to their price instead, or decline; price integrity across customers protects every later deal. [practitioner]

## Prospect says "just send me a proposal"

- Default: test whether it is a brush-off or a real step: confirm what decision the proposal will enable and when; a proposal with no agreed decision criteria is a stall. [practitioner]
- Write it around the explicit needs in their words; how many options to show and which to recommend is in `references/offer-positioning.md`, section Building a three-tier proposal. [practitioner]
- Exception: when discovery has not happened, trade the proposal for a 20-minute scoping call; otherwise it answers questions they never asked (SPIN). [data]
- Script: "Happy to. So it lands right: if the proposal covers [need A] and [need B] at roughly the figure we discussed, what happens next on your side, and when?" [practitioner]
- Mistake: sending the proposal and waiting → put the buyer's next action and the review date in the cover note; see `references/closing.md`, section Proposal sent, now what. [data]

## Pre-empting the objection you know is coming

- Raise the likely objection yourself and refute it in the same breath ("you'll hear we're the priciest; here's what that buys"); naming it unrefuted persuades less than silence (O'Keefe 1999; Allen 1991). [study]
- Inoculate against the competitor's attack with a weakened version of it plus your refutation; this beats restating your strengths (Banas & Rains 2010, 54 cases). [study]
- Time it: inoculation holds for moderate delays and decays after about two weeks, so refresh the refutation close to the decision meeting (Banas & Rains 2010). [study]
- Mistake: telegraphing persuasion ("let me address your price concerns") or announcing the pitch days ahead → go straight to the point; forewarning breeds counter-arguments and bolstering (Wood & Quinn 2003; Fransen 2015). [study]
- Say your weak spot first, specifically, then why it does not matter for their case; a flawless pitch is discounted because buyers assume competitors exist. [practitioner]
- Pre-empt the follow-up while answering: "people in your role usually ask X next, so here's that answer" signals you have worked with buyers like them (JOLT). [data]
- Clear known obstacles (other deciders, timing, fit) before you present price; after the ask they become objections, which is harder. [practitioner]
- Invite the objection right after the pitch ("what feels off?", "what reservations do you have?") rather than waiting for the stall; unspoken objections don't vanish. [practitioner]
- Give the champion a one-paragraph written refutation to repeat internally; talking about the threat strengthens inoculation (Ivanov et al. 2012, via Fransen 2015). [study]

## Handling price pushback and discount requests

Timing, from Gong call data (correlational, Gong customers only):

- Discuss price in the first call: win rates by first price discussion were 42% (first call), 32% (second), 15% (third), 5% (never), whoever raised it (Gong 2020, 11,331 opportunities). [data]
- Default: raise price on the first call, after value, about three-quarters in (Gong 2020, 38–46 minutes, 11,331 opportunities); asked earlier, give a range and return to it. Exception: published pricing, skip to the value reason. [data]

Discount pushback and procurement:

- Trade every concession and change scope or terms rather than price; the concession rules and Huthwaite negotiator behaviours live in `references/closing.md`, section Concessions. [practitioner]
- Answer a pilot or termination-for-convenience request by noting it removes the commitment the price assumed; point to warranty, SLA and termination-for-cause protections. [practitioner]
- When procurement escalates ("your CFO makes exceptions"), take the conversation, state that every customer pays the same, and hand them the levers (term, volume, timing) as their cards. [practitioner]
- Walking away or "sharpen your pencil" costs the buyer nothing; rather than chasing with a new number, restate the trade and let their own deadline do the work. [practitioner]
- Open with a precise, reasoned number inside the zone of agreement; first-offer and counter-anchor rules live in `references/closing.md`, section First offers and anchors. [study]

Voss and Huthwaite tools for the conversation itself:

- Default: label the concern or stance tentatively ("sounds like the number is above what you'd planned"), not the emotion. Exception: in a hostile moment use no emotion word at all. [practitioner]
- After the label, wait; aim for "that's right", not "you're right", which means go away. [practitioner]
- Ask calibrated how/what questions ("how am I supposed to get there?", "what about this doesn't work for you?") instead of offering a counter-number. [practitioner]
- Voice your position as a feeling before disagreeing ("I'm worried we're far apart on this") rather than "I disagree because"; skilled negotiators lead up to disagreement (Huthwaite, 103 sessions). [data]
- Mistake: defend/attack replies ("you can't blame us for that") → restate their point, then your position as a feeling; defend/attack starts a spiral (1.9% vs 6.3% of comments, Huthwaite). [data]

## Worked examples

### Email reply: "Thanks, but we already use [Vendor]. Not interested."

Diagnosis: a reason with substance (a named vendor) plus reluctance to change. No pitch, no rebuttal, one problem question, an explicit out.

Reply: "Fair enough, and thanks for replying; most people don't. One question, then I'll leave you alone: teams on [Vendor] usually tell us [specific gap] is still handled by hand in a spreadsheet. If that's not the case for you, I'll close the file. If it is, is it worth 15 minutes to see how [peer company] removed it?"

Why it works: treats the vendor as a truth, names one specific gap, hands back the decision, makes one ask.

### "Think it over" after an agreed ROI case

Context: a VP agreed the ROI math and saw pricing, then said "this looks right, let me think it over." Value is agreed, no question is open: indecision, not status quo.

Response: "Of course. Is there a question still open, or is it more about being sure it's the right call? If the latter, I'd start with [team A] on a 90-day term with an exit clause; here's why."

Why it works: judges the indecision, recommends one option, narrows scope, removes risk. No "what will waiting cost you", which would make an indecisive buyer more indecisive.

### Price pushback on a services proposal

Context: a 60k three-phase services proposal; the buyer says "that's about 40% over what we budgeted, can you do 45?" A number means a negotiation, not a value objection: hold price integrity, trade scope or terms.

Response: "I can get to 45, not by discounting the same scope though. Which of the three phases matters least this quarter? We can hold it to phase two, or keep all three at 60 with payment over two quarters."

Why it works: no instant counter-number, concession traded for scope or terms, two options with one implied recommendation.

## Common mistakes and replacements

- Answering the objection the moment it lands with a 21-second justification → pause, one clarifying question, then one reason (Gong 2018, 67,149 demos). [data]
- Treating "I need to think about it" as avoidance to break with pain and urgency → judge indecision first; recommend, limit exploration, remove risk (JOLT). [data]
- Stacking four price angles or memorised stories → the single strongest reason tied to the need they named (Weaver, Garcia & Schwarz 2012). [study]
- Mentioning a weakness or "I'll probably be the highest bid" without the reason → attach the refutation in the same sentence (O'Keefe 1999). [study]
- Announcing "let me address your concerns about price" → go straight to the point; telegraphing persuasion triggers defensive bolstering (Wood & Quinn 2003). [study]
- Repeating closing techniques after pushback in a considered purchase → propose one realistic advance and book it; repeated closes lowered trust and success (Hawes, Strong & Winick 1996; SPIN). [study]
- Pitching against the incumbent's weaknesses → one problem question about the gap; attacking a vendor they chose triggers biased processing in its favour (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Hearing "you're right" as a win → it means go away; summarise their view until you hear "that's right". [practitioner]
- Answering an ultimatum with "never", or issuing one you won't keep → "given where things are, that would be very hard for you"; ultimatums trigger reactance. [practitioner]
