# Rapport, openers and trust

Open this file for first impressions, opening a call or meeting, building trust, small talk, listening, and a cold or hostile prospect. Generic rapport advice is assumed known; only the delta is here. Scripts are at most 40 words. Vendor call data are correlational. Numbers with sample and caveat: `references/benchmarks.md`; who said it: `references/sources.md`.

## Rules the model gets wrong

- Spend effort on responding, not the opener: in recorded B2B first meetings seller-initiated moves had little effect; continuing the buyer's topic, echoing it in your words and confirming understanding did (Kaski 2018, 7 meetings). [study]
- Use rapport to earn disclosure and future business, not today's decision: trust raised expected future business but not the current supplier choice, once performance and experience were controlled (Doney & Cannon 1997, 200+ buyers). [study]
- Open with a reason or permission, not "did I catch you at a bad time?" (2.15% versus 11.24% at best, Gong 2024, 300M+ calls); "how are you?" is fine (5.2% versus 1.5%, Gong 2018). [data]
- Lead with integrity and competence cues, not warmth displays: moral character outranks warmth in first impressions (Goodwin 2014), and a broad smile cut perceived competence and purchase intention in high-risk purchases (Wang 2017). [study]
- Remove even minimal hostility, sarcasm or correction before adding any technique: in field interviews, minimal hostile behaviour reduced information yield while motivational-interviewing style raised it (Alison 2013). [study]
- AI seller: show specific knowledge and empathy in the first turns; a bot disclosed up front cut purchases 79.7% because it seemed less knowledgeable and empathetic (Luo 2019, 6,200+ customers). Concealment is not the lesson. [study]

## Opening a cold call

- Put a trigger before the permission line; permission openers booked 11.18% (Gong 2024). Opener script in `references/outreach.md`, section "Scripts". [data]
- Retire stock permission lines ("I'm making a cold call, can I have 30 seconds?"): they now mark you as a telemarketer; the specific trigger is what escapes the stigma. [practitioner]
- State the reason for the call within the first sentences; calls that gave a reason booked 2.1x the baseline (Gong 2018, 90,380 calls). [data]
- Keep pleasantries brief and ordinary; "how have you been?" booked 10.01% versus a 1.5% baseline (Gong 2018) and "how's your day going?" 7.6% (Gong 2024). [data]
- Follow the opener with one specific, customer-language problem and a neutral question ("how are you handling X today?"), then stop talking. [practitioner]
- Say the human thing when brushed off: "you probably don't like these calls, and frankly I don't like making them"; a faceless telemarketer gets dismissed. [practitioner]

## Opening a first meeting

- Default: keep preliminaries short; openings mattered less than expected in large sales (SPIN/Huthwaite). Exception: when the buyer opens with small talk, continue it; the worst-rated recorded meeting skipped it for slides (Kaski 2018). [data]
- If you open the small talk, make it original and personal rather than cliché; clichéd small talk read as false friendliness in recorded meetings (Kaski 2018). [study]
- Prepare two or three topics beforehand, even for 30 seconds, or you default to the food or the room; Brooks reports experiments where topic prep made conversations less anxious and more enjoyable. [practitioner]
- Raise a likely concern up front, such as an existing contract with a rival; sellers who did so in recordings drew runs of agreement rather than resistance (Kaski 2018). [study]
- Ask permission for a run of direct questions after three or four minutes, framed like a doctor's bedside manner; skilled negotiators labelled questions in advance 6.4% versus 1.2% (Rackham & Carlisle 1978). [study]
- Treat the buyer as the expert on their business and say so; buyers judged rapport by whether their status and competence were acknowledged (Jokiniemi 2023, 38 interviewees). [study]

## The hostile or curt buyer

- Diagnose: curt but answering is fine, move to problem questions (`discovery.md`); hostile or guarded means fix the atmosphere before any question list. [practitioner]
- Expect inherited suspicion: one deceptive experience makes people sceptical of unrelated sellers, so a cold reception is often about the last vendor (Fransen 2015 review). [study]
- Time-box: "Give me five minutes to say why I'm here, then we discuss for ten, and you decide." [practitioner]
- Ask why they agreed to meet before pushing questions; wording and follow-ups in `references/discovery.md`, section "The buyer who answers in one word". [practitioner]
- When they doubt your credentials, acknowledge it out loud, take yourself off the pedestal and invite them to teach you; hostile written feedback turned "interesting" after one such opener. [practitioner]
- Label the stance tentatively (Voss label: "sounds like you've been burned by vendors before"), not the emotion; the emotion-labelling rule and "that's right" test are in `references/objections.md`. [practitioner]
- Offer choices about what to cover and in what order; interviewees who felt in control gave more, and "regaining control" gave less. [practitioner]
- Fix your own weakness before adding technique: one sarcastic or critical turn can wreck the meeting however good the rest. [practitioner]

## Re-opening after silence

- In-call silence: wait; buyer types read silence as a cue to talk, as thinking time, or as anger, so filling it with a pitch misreads two of three (Voss). [practitioner]
- Pause at the end of a clause to invite "yeah" and "okay"; pauses that let the partner chime in raised perceived helpfulness and vanished when they could not (Zant 2025, 200 calls). [study]
- After days of silence, ask the either-or that lets them say no; wording in `references/outreach.md`, section "Prospect went dark after a conversation". [practitioner]
- When rapport has slipped, go back a stage: respond to the topic they raise instead of your agenda; customers withhold when rapport drops and open up as it rebuilds (Jokiniemi 2023). [study]

## User asks for rapport techniques (mirroring, compliments, common ground)

- Respond to every topic the buyer introduces, even one that seems trivial; continuing their line of action built rapport where seller-started lines did not (Kaski 2018). [study]
- Echo the upshot of what they said in your own words and invite confirmation; it shows understanding rather than claiming it (Kaski 2018). [study]
- Add something to each agreement, a matching experience or an empathetic reaction; agreeing with everything lost credibility in recordings (Kaski 2018). [study]
- Ask follow-up questions; askers were liked more and learned more, and nobody, askers included, predicted it (Huang 2017; Gino co-author, flag when citing). [study]
- Go one level deeper than small talk when they let you; strangers underestimated how well deep questions would land (Kardas 2022, 12 experiments). [study]
- Put attention on follow-ups rather than deliberate mirroring: a preregistered test found no effect of posture mimicry on rapport, liking or trust (Hale 2016); earlier positive studies were small. [study; mixed replication]
- Use incidental similarity (shared town, birthday) only while behaving well; the gain in purchase intention reversed when the seller behaved badly (Jiang 2010). [study]
- Lead with specific knowledge of their business; selling-related knowledge was the strongest predictor of performance across 268 studies (Verbeke 2011). [study]
- Name a weakness early only with its refutation attached; the two-sided form and wording are in `references/objections.md`, section "Pre-empting the objection you know is coming". [study]
- Expect listening to pay through the relationship: perceived listening correlated .51 with relationship quality and .36 with performance (Kluger 2023, 664 effects, mostly self-report). [study]

## Scripts

- First-meeting permission: "Would it help if I asked direct questions for ten minutes, the way a doctor would, and you flag anything confidential? Then I'll tell you what I think." [practitioner]
- Echo and confirm: "So, if I've got it: every quarter the report lands late and you rebuild it by hand. Did I get that right, or what did I miss?" [study]

## Common mistakes and replacements

- "Did I catch you at a bad time?" or "is now a bad time?" → trigger plus permission, or the reason for the call (Gong 2018, 2024). [data]
- Skipping "how are you?" because it "lowers trust" → brief pleasantries are fine; the line booked 5.2% versus a 1.5% baseline (Gong 2018). [data]
- Name, compliment, common-ground checklist as the opener → respond to what they raise, echo it, add a matching experience (Kaski 2018). [study]
- Deliberate mirroring of posture or phrasing → follow-up questions and summaries; the preregistered mimicry test found no rapport or trust effect (Hale 2016). [study]
- Big smile and high enthusiasm on a high-stakes deal → composed delivery with competence cues first; broad smiles cut competence and intention when risk was high (Wang 2017). [study]
- Passion-first opener about the sailboat photos → let them start small talk; keep preliminaries short in large sales (SPIN/Huthwaite; Kaski 2018). [data]
- Aiming to be liked as the closing lever → win today on diagnosed value; trust predicts future business more than the current choice (Doney & Cannon 1997). [study]
- Hiding that the seller is an AI → disclose where required and show specific knowledge and empathy in the first turns (Luo 2019). [study]
- Sarcasm, sharp correction or "I need to regain control" → remove the hostile turn first; minimal hostility cut disclosure (Alison 2013). [study]
- Talking 10% or aiming for 2:1 listening → treat the ratio as a diagnostic; see `discovery.md`. [data]
