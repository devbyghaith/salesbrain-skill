## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Keep a cold email under 75 words at grade 3–7, one interest-based ask: interest CTAs 30% vs time-specific 15% (Gong); 0–50 words +23%, grade 3–7 1.66x (Lavender 2026); vendor stats, no samples stated [data]
- Leave ROI claims and social proof out of a first touch: ROI language cut cold-email success 15%; social proof linked to 22% lower close rates, 47% on early calls (Gong, vendor stat, no sample stated) [data]
- Cut the word "AI" from outreach copy and describe the outcome or the problem instead; Jason Bay reports "AI" was among the biggest drags on reply rate in his cold-email data [practitioner]
- Personalize so the message changes: if removing the cue leaves the email intact, it was not personalization; a name in the subject alone raised leads 31%, cut unsubscribes 17% (Sahni 2018, field RCTs) [study]
- Count the denominator before quoting a reply rate: 7.5M cold emails in 2025 drew 0.45% replies per send, and a "5% reply rate" on opens equals about 0.45% on sends (Belkins) [data]
- Treat cadence as having a hard stop: Gmail rejects senders whose spam-complaint rate passes 0.3% (Google sender guidelines, 2024), so over-sequencing costs the whole domain, not one prospect [data]
- Work an inbound lead within the hour: firms that did were about 7x as likely to qualify it (Oldroyd 2011, 2,241 firms), and about 70% of marketing leads are never pursued (Sabnis 2013) [study]
- Keep cold calling in the B2B mix: opener success ranged 2.15%–11.24% across 300M+ calls, "did I catch you at a bad time?" lowest; two-thirds of buyers accept cold calls (Gong 2024; RAIN 2018) [data]
- Aim outreach at getting on the shortlist, not closing: 94% of buying groups ranked vendors before first contact and the pre-contact favourite won 77% of the time (6sense 2025, ~4,000 buyers, vendor survey) [data]
- Plan for 5–8 touches, not 12+: top performers converted prospects in 5 touches versus 8 for the rest (RAIN 2018, 489 sellers, self-report survey); enterprise exception in the cadence section [data]

## Choosing who to target

- Pick one "who" that passes three tests: pain strong enough to act, capacity to pay, and a renewing supply of similar buyers; declining industries fail the third [practitioner]
- Target pain the buyer already feels; manufacturing pain for a business happily serving customers ("your logo sucks") produces resistance, not demand [practitioner]
- Find buyers at the "today's the day" moment (a trigger: new office, funding, hire, tool change) rather than assuming a fixed share of any list converts [practitioner]
- Separate payer from user: when someone else pays (employer, parent), the sale is easier and refunds rarer; market to the payer and arm the user with the arguments [practitioner]
- Add conditions to the dream client beyond industry: established and funded over pre-revenue, second project over first, buyers who have bought this category before [practitioner]
- Treat each segment as a hypothesis: hand-find 30 people worth 15 minutes each, send across email, LinkedIn and phone, count replies, then change message or role before adding tools [practitioner]
- If 30 such people are hard to find, the segment is undiscoverable; pick another before building outbound, because qualifying on calls collapses conversion [practitioner]
- Judge a weak outbound result as a message or unfelt-problem failure before a tooling failure; the same tools and effort swing several-fold on the insight in the note [practitioner]
- If every prospect who meets you loves you and closes, positioning is fine and you need more at-bats; if first calls show confused faces, fix positioning before campaigns [practitioner]
- Segment call and email lists by job to be done, not mixed titles: a CFO cares about cost per unit, a marketing director about speed; one mixed list forces a generic talk track [practitioner]
- Expect replies to fall with seniority and size: founders 0.57%, C-level 0.42%, VPs 0.32%; 0–10 employees 0.72%, 10,000+ 0.22% (Belkins 2025, per send) [data]

## Writing a first-touch cold email

- Structure in three parts, 4–5 sentences, no scrolling on mobile: why you, in your role, now (relevance); the problem and the cost of doing nothing; one interest question [practitioner]
- Keep the subject to 2–4 plain words that withhold the payoff ("your streaming bill"), not a promotion; one-to-three-word subjects performed best (Lavender via practitioner, six million emails claimed, no method stated) [data]
- Phrase the one ask as interest ("worth a look sometime?", "open to hearing how?"), not a time ("15 minutes Tuesday?") and not open-ended ("thoughts?"), which scored lowest at 13% (Gong, vendor stat, no sample stated) [data]
- Send the first touch as plain text with no links, images or attachments, for deliverability; once it has landed, the second email can carry a link or short video [practitioner]
- Say nothing about yourself or how the product works in the first email; each sentence's only job is to get the next one read, and the mechanism belongs in the conversation [practitioner]
- Lead with a counter-intuitive insight about their situation rather than "better, faster, cheaper"; buyers getting the job done today answer "better" with "not interested" [practitioner]
- Poke the bear: label a likely situation ("looks like you just cut the cord"), then ask a neutral question about the cost of carrying on; a wrong label still gets a correcting reply [practitioner]
- Default: lead with relevance (why this role, this company, now) over flattery ("loved your post... anyway, we sell CRMs"). Exception: a personal detail that ladders to the problem can go in a P.S. [practitioner]
- Take problem language from customer quotes in case studies and G2 reviews ("nightmare", "overpaying 15–18%"), not marketing copy; round "5x/10x" claims read as invented [practitioner]
- Put one problem per email and move one chess move at a time; listing every problem you solve (swim, bike, run, nutrition) overloads the reader [practitioner]
- Use "you" in the subject and first line: "tips to save you money" drew more engagement than "tips to save money" in research a marketing professor cites; no study named [practitioner]
- Use AI to research and draft; an AI sender disclosed up front cut purchases 79.7% (Luo 2019), and the mitigation is in `references/rapport.md`, Rules the model gets wrong [study]

### Three-part template (under 75 words)

> subject: [2–4 words on their situation]
>
> [Name], [trigger or role-specific observation: why them, now]. [What usually goes wrong next for people in that spot,
> in their own words, and what carrying on costs]. [One interest question: "how are you handling that today?" or
> "worth a look sometime?"]

## Making a cold call

- Choose a permission-based or name-mention opener: 11.18% and 11.24% success versus 2.15% for "bad time?" and 7.6% for "how's your day going?" (Gong 2024, 300M+ calls, success undefined) [data]
- Keep a brief pleasantry: "how are you?" booked meetings at 5.2% versus a 1.5% baseline (Gong 2018, 90,380 calls); the claim that it lowers trust has no data behind it [data]
- Put a trigger before the permission ("just read about your Tacoma office; this is a cold call, mind if I take thirty seconds to say why?"); a stock permission line alone reads as telemarketing [practitioner]
- Sell time, not product, in the first 20 seconds: the first line buys the next 20 seconds, those buy two minutes, and two minutes earn a discovery call [practitioner]
- Run the problem-question structure after the opener: optional softener ("not sure about you, but..."), one specific fact in customer words, a neutral question ("how are you handling that today?"), then silence [practitioner]
- Lead with the problem, not the product: "keeps water cold three days" is a product message; "your cold drinks don't stay cold" names what happens if nothing changes [practitioner]
- Deliver the words casually, as if you expect nothing; memorize the script first so attention can go to tone, because a punchy pitch tone triggers the "not interested" reflex [practitioner]
- Add "if you don't mind me asking" before the question; it hands control to the prospect, lowers resistance and makes "not interested" harder to say [practitioner]
- Use honest context only; "I sent you something, did you get it?" when you did not is the stock dishonesty, and honesty itself now differentiates [practitioner]
- Call in one morning block of 20–40 dials to one persona with a prepared message; scattered calls through the day keep you anxious and the list unsorted [practitioner]
- Expect list quality, not talk quality, to explain most of the gap: upper-quartile reps connect on 13.3% of dials versus a 5.5% median, so prioritize direct and mobile numbers (Gong 2024, 300M+ calls, via practitioners) [data]
- Put calls in every sequence: phone-centric SDR teams logged 4.6 quality conversations a day versus 3.4 for email-centric ones (Bridge Group 2025, 351 firms, self-report survey) [data]
- Leave a voicemail that points to the email and asks for a thumbs up or down, not a callback; calls alongside emails lifted replies from 1.81% to 3.44% (Gong via practitioners, no sample stated) [data]
- With a gatekeeper, ask for the person with minimum information in a familiar tone ("could you put me through to John? It's Nick"); lying, befriending or pitching them all fail [practitioner]
- Drill the opener in role-play, not live: a drill partner gives dozens of simulated calls an hour, while a month of real conversations is too few to test anything [practitioner]

## Prospect says "not interested" or "send me some info"

- Treat a reasonless brush-off as a reflex to the interruption, not a verdict; about half of cold-call objections are rejection reflexes (practitioner estimate), so agree first, then ask with an incentive [practitioner]
- "Not interested": pause two beats, then: "That's okay, I know you didn't ask me to call. Before we hang up, if you don't mind me asking, is it my timing or is this irrelevant?" [practitioner]
- "Send me some info": "So I don't send you something irrelevant, would it be okay if I ask two quick questions?" Then qualify; a reasonless "send info" is usually a brush-off [practitioner]
- "We're already covered": "Makes sense. So nobody on my team calls again, is it that you already have something, we're not addressing an issue you care about, or you just hate cold calls?" [practitioner]
- Once a real objection surfaces behind the brush-off (price, current vendor, timing), switch to its section in `references/objections.md`; the three steps are agree, incentive question, real objection [practitioner]

## Building a cadence: touches, spacing, stop conditions

- Default: 5–8 touches over about three weeks across email, phone and LinkedIn, at most three emails. Exception: enterprise, when a measured profit-per-lead curve supports more (`references/sales-process-team.md`, Designing or fixing the sales process) [data]
- Expect the first email and first follow-up to carry most replies: the first follow-up lifted replies 49% and four or more emails raised unsubscribes and spam complaints (Belkins 16.5M emails, secondary summary) [data]
- Pause after the day-1 email and two calls until day 8, then shrink the gaps (8, 13, 17, 20): a personalized first impression needs room, and tapering sequences teach prospects that waiting you out works [practitioner]
- Stop at an explicit no, an unsubscribe, a spam flag, or after the breakup touch; keep your word and stay away about six weeks before any new reason to write [practitioner]
- Automate only after a hand-sent batch gets replies; blasting 5,000 notes in a day can damage the domain before the message is right [practitioner]
- Send 8am–12pm recipient time and midweek when nothing else decides it: those slots drew 0.54% and 0.48% replies against a 0.45% average (Belkins 2025, 7.5M emails) [data]
- Write the breakup as a release, not guilt: say you will stop, name the problem once more, leave one interest question; a list of ignored touches reads as a scolding [practitioner]

Example sequence, 8 touches and 3 emails over 20 days, gaps shrinking after the first pause [practitioner]:

| Day | Touch | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | email 1, then a call (voicemail points to the email) | relevance, one problem, one interest question |
| 2 | call | same premise as email 1; then leave them alone until day 8 |
| 8 | email 2 as a reply in the thread, then a call | second problem, or a new metaphor for the first |
| 13 | LinkedIn: comment on their post or connection note (see Scripts) | no pitch; a fresh trigger or a customer phrase |
| 17 | call with voicemail | "thumbs up or down on the email?" |
| 20 | breakup email | release, problem once more, one question; then silence |

## Prospect went dark after a conversation

- Ask the timing-or-relevance question once: "is this a timing thing, or has it dropped off the list?" The answer tells you whether to nurture or close the file [practitioner]
- Re-open with something that changed on their side (a hire, a posting, a deadline) or a changed assumption of yours, keeping the ask to one line; "checking in" offers nothing [practitioner]
- Read silence after a push as possible reactance: pressing a resistant buyer can entrench the original view (Fransen 2015, review), so lower the ask rather than raise the pressure [study]
- Use the breakup pattern after two unanswered re-engagements; silence is the one objection you cannot counter, so give the prospect an easy way to say no [practitioner]
- Prevent going dark up front: end every meeting with the next one booked, calendars open, and ask what a delay costs them ("book a meeting from a meeting") [practitioner]

## Reaching out on LinkedIn

- Start the sequence with a profile view: confirm they are still in a relevant role and pull three premises (angles) to use across later touches [practitioner]
- Write a DM like the email (3–4 sentences, relevance, problem, one question) and send it from the founder when there is one; the market wants the person who knows something it doesn't [practitioner]
- Earn the right to be heard before the approach: avoidance happens before exposure, so a comment on their post or a shared group beats an unprompted pitch (Fransen 2015, review) [study]
- Budget LinkedIn as one channel among three, behind phone and email; the measured SDR activity split is in `references/sales-process-team.md`, Operating benchmarks for an SDR team [data]

## Inbound lead arrives

- Route by segment and engagement: enterprise and VP-level leads get a call on first site touch; mid-market once email and phone are given; small business nurture to a group demo [practitioner]
- Tune the hand-off line from close rates: sales closing almost none of the passed leads means the line is too early; closing nearly all means too late and money left on the table [practitioner]
- Fix follow-up with prequalification and manager tracking, not exhortation: those two, plus rep experience, drove whether reps pursued leads (Sabnis 2013, 461 reps, 4 firms) [study]
- Open an inbound call with "what motivated you to look into us?" then go back to when the problem was first defined; that question confuses an outbound prospect, who never chose you [practitioner]

## Asking a customer for a referral

- Expect referred customers to be worth at least 16% more than comparable non-referred ones, with higher retention (Schmitt 2011, ~10,000 bank customers, 3 years); "referrals close at 80–90%" has no source [study]
- Ask selectively: the customers with the highest referral or reference value are not the highest-revenue ones; relationship length and congruence with the prospect matter more (Kumar 2013, B2B) [study]
- Ask with a name-forcing question, not a yes/no: "Who do you know who's in the same spot you were six months ago?" then wait; "do you know anyone?" invites "no" [practitioner]
- Ask right after a delivered result and hand the referrer a forwardable two-line note; even a lost prospect who felt cared for will introduce you [practitioner]
- Lead with the referrer's words, not yours: people trust commercial sources less than other customers, so a referred intro carries credibility your claims cannot (Fransen 2015, review) [study]

## Lead magnets, assessments and events

- Run a short online assessment on your niche (about 15 questions) and link every piece of content to it; the next step after any result is a one-to-one appointment (small-ticket, single-call) [practitioner]
- Hold a live "introduction to X" session on a two-week cycle (fill, follow up, deliver, follow up), on what buyers wish they could learn fast, not how to do your craft [practitioner]
- Fill a small event by warm outreach to researched people; Daniel Priestley reports 50–100 invitations for a 5–6 person session, with qualifying questions on the booking form [practitioner]
- Tie every lead magnet to the target's felt pain; a magnet built around your craft attracts peers and students, not buyers [practitioner]
- Give a registration-of-interest form a handful of qualifying questions (budget, scope known, in-house person) so a stranger can self-signal a project at any hour [practitioner]
- Go where target users concentrate when email fails: one startup got dozens of fire-chief meetings by walking into stations; another met users in a conference coffee shop for $0 [practitioner]

## Scripts

Spoken scripts stay under 40 words, written ones under 75. Fill the brackets from the prospect's situation; mark gaps
`[NEEDS INPUT]` rather than inventing a trigger, customer or number.

### Cold-call opener, trigger then permission (spoken) [practitioner]

> [Name], it's [me] at [company]. I just read about [trigger]; this is a cold call, but a researched one. Mind if I take
> thirty seconds to say why that led me to you?

### Cold-call first question (spoken) [practitioner]

> Not sure about your team, but [peer group] usually tell us [specific problem in their words]. If you don't mind me
> asking, how are you handling that today?

### Voicemail (spoken) [practitioner]

> Hi [Name], it's [me] at [company]. No need to call back. I'm sending you an email about [problem]; would you mind a
> thumbs up or down on it? Thanks.

### LinkedIn connection note (written, under 40 words, no pitch) [practitioner]

> [Name], saw [trigger: your post on X]. I work with [peer role] on [problem area] and your angle on [topic] was a
> new one to me. Glad to connect either way.

### Referral ask (spoken) [practitioner]

> You mentioned [result] landed well. Who do you know who's where you were six months ago, wrestling with [problem]?
> If a name comes to mind, I'll write a two-line note you can forward or ignore.

### Breakup email (written) [practitioner]

> subject: closing the file
>
> [Name], I'll stop here. If [problem] isn't on your plate this quarter, no reply needed and I'll leave you alone. If it
> is, a one-word answer tells me whether to send how [peer type] handle it. Either way, thanks for the inbox space.

### Warm event invitation (written) [practitioner]

> We're running an introduction to [topic] on Zoom next [day], free, six people. It's for [role] who [situation].
> Noticed you [trigger]; thought it might be useful. Want the link?

## Worked examples

### First-touch cold email

Trigger: a 60-person software firm posted three SDR openings. Seller: call-coaching software.

> subject: three new SDRs
>
> Hi Dana, saw the three SDR openings. Teams hiring their first SDRs usually find out around month three that the reps
> who sound busiest book the fewest meetings, and nobody can hear why. How are you planning to listen to their calls,
> or is that further down the list?

Why it works: 49 words, one problem, one interest question; the trigger changes the ask, so it passes the removal
test; nothing about the product, no proof, no "AI", no link.

### Re-engagement after a prospect went dark

Context: discovery call in March, two unanswered follow-ups since.

> subject: four more SDR roles
>
> Hi Dana, closing the loop on our March call. Since then your team posted four more SDR roles, which usually turns
> "listen to calls eventually" into a ramp deadline. Is SDR ramp still on your list this quarter, or has it dropped
> off? A one-word answer is fine either way.

Why it works: the reason is a change on their side, not "checking in"; the timing-or-relevance question gives an easy
no; the ask is one line.

### Cold-call opener plus first question

Same trigger, by phone.

> Hi Dana, it's Sam at Acme. I just read about the three SDR openings; this is a cold call, but a researched one. Mind
> if I take thirty seconds to say why that led me to you?

On "go ahead":

> Not sure about your team, but first-time SDR managers usually tell us the reps who sound busiest book the fewest
> meetings, and nobody can hear why. If you don't mind me asking, how are you listening to their calls today?

Then silence. Why it works: trigger before permission, problem before product, a neutral question that hands control
to the prospect; 37 and 40 spoken words.

## Common mistakes and replacements

| Mistake | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| 150-word email, three paragraphs, calendar link | Under 75 words, one interest question, plain text, no links in the first touch |
| "Do you have 15 minutes Tuesday?" as the first ask | "Worth a look sometime?" or "how are you handling that today?" (interest 30% vs time 15%, Gong, no sample stated) |
| ROI claims and "trusted by 3,000 teams" in the first touch | Name the problem in customer words; keep proof for when they ask |
| "AI-powered" in the subject or opening line | Describe the outcome or the problem; leave "AI" out of outreach copy |
| "Loved your post on leadership! Anyway, we sell CRMs" | A cue that changes the ask, or no personal cue at all; relevance over flattery |
| Quoting a "5–8% reply rate" benchmark | 0.45% per send across 7.5M emails (Belkins 2025); state the denominator every time |
| "Did I catch you at a bad time?" | Trigger, then permission or reason for the call |
| Pitching harder after "not interested" | Pause, then "is it my timing, or is this irrelevant?" |
| "Just checking in" or "bumping this to the top" | A change on their side, a second problem, or the breakup email |
| 12-touch email-only sequence blasted to 5,000 contacts | 5–8 mixed touches, at most three emails; the domain's 0.3% spam ceiling is the limit |
| Working inbound leads the next morning | Within the hour; enterprise and VP leads get a call on first touch |
| "Referrals close at 80–90%, ask everyone" | At least 16% more valuable (Schmitt 2011); ask selectively with a name-forcing question |
