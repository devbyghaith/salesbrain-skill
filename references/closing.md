## Rules the model usually gets wrong

- Replace the close with an advance, a buyer action that moves the deal. Closing techniques help only in small sales; beyond petty-cash purchases they cut success (SPIN/Huthwaite, 35,000+ call observations). [data]
- Expect every named closing technique to cost trust: in the one direct test, all five techniques scored below no close, though none caused outright distrust (Hawes, Strong & Winick 1996, abstract only). [study]
- End every substantive call with a specific, buyer-owned, dated next step: fastest deals spent 53% more first-meeting time on them, and close rates fell 71% without first-call next steps (Gong 2018, 28,833 deals). [data]
- Diagnose indecision and de-risk it instead of adding pressure: fear tactics on a hesitating buyer raised no-decision odds 84% of the time (JOLT, 2.5M conversations); diagnosis and reply in `references/objections.md`. [data]
- Treat a verbal yes as words, not behaviour: door-in-the-face raised verbal compliance (r = .126) but not behavioural compliance (r = .052, not significant) (Feeley, Anker & Aloe 2012 meta-analysis). [study]
- Use autonomy language as respect, not a lever: "but you are free" showed no effect in the seven low-bias tests (g = 0.11, Fillon 2023), and consistency tactics failed replication (Vaidis 2024). [study; mixed replication]
- Raise price on the first call, after value, about three-quarters in (Gong 2020, 11,331 opportunities); timing rules in `references/objections.md`, section Handling price pushback and discount requests. [data]

## Which motion are you in

Pick the row before giving closing advice; the rows differ on pressure tolerance, who owns the next step, and whether to ask for the order on the call.

| Motion | Pressure tolerance | Who owns the next step | Ask for the order on this call? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-call, small-ticket (one signer who can decide today) | Highest, yet named closes still cost trust (Hawes 1996) | Seller proposes; buyer accepts or declines now | Yes, once the buyer has named the need and the money |
| Multi-touch complex B2B (committee, evaluation, paper process) | Low; pressure raises no-decision odds (JOLT) | Buyer, with a date; seller brings 2–3 options | No; ask for the highest realistic advance |
| Founder-led enterprise (first customers, no process yet) | Low; the mature buyer often dictates the steps | Buyer books the next call and names who else attends | No; ask for the second call and the names |

- Default: ask for an advance, not the order. Exception: when the buyer can sign today and has stated need and budget, ask for the order (SPIN small-sale finding). [data]
- Treat "ask for the order" tactics as single-call tools; in complex multi-issue deals even the price anchor weakens (Petrowsky 2025, effects shrink as issues multiply). [study]
- Bring 2–3 specific next-step options to a first meeting; next-step time in later calls did not shorten cycles (Gong 2018, 28,833 deals). [data]

### Which qualification method to run

No outcome evidence compares these frameworks; this table is practitioner grade and chosen by deal shape, not by preference.

| Condition you can observe | Run | Check before asking for commitment |
|---|---|---|
| One signer, short cycle, buyer already solution-aware | BANT, four questions | Budget exists, this person can sign, the need is explicit, the timing is theirs not yours |
| Buying committee, formal evaluation, paper process, a renewal or incumbent at stake | MEDDICC | Economic buyer named, decision criteria and process written down, paper process mapped, champion tested, pain quantified |
| Buyer not yet convinced to change; "do nothing" is the main rival | Gap or jobs-to-be-done questions plus the three whys | What they do now vs what they want, cost of staying, why buy, why us, why now |

- Qualify decision-making ability as well as buying ability: a buyer who cannot decide is disqualified or forecast later, however good the fit (JOLT). [data]
- Run the status-quo check before the committee map: without a negative consequence of waiting, a fully mapped committee still ends in no decision (three whys). [practitioner]

## Ending a discovery call

- Propose the highest realistic commitment the buyer can give, then settle one step down; top performers often get the first ask because explicit needs were established (SPIN/Huthwaite). [data]
- Set up the next step in the opening: "toward the end, if this looks like a fit, we can talk about possible next steps" lowers resistance to discussing them later. [practitioner]
- Ask for a self-prediction, not an intention: "will you have the three stakeholders on Thursday's call?" beat intention questions (d+ = 0.29 vs 0.12; Wilding 2016, 94 tests). [study]
- Ask the commitment question live and close to the action: question effects were largest face to face (g = 0.23), smallest by mail (g = 0.06), and faded with delay (Wilding 2016). [study]
- Book the next meeting with calendars open and ask who else should attend; a buyer who will not give calendar time is usually declining politely. [practitioner]
- Confirm the agreed step in writing the same day: recollections drift once the call ends, and the note is where you ask what was forgotten (owner, date). [practitioner]
- Spoken close: "Based on what you described, the useful next step is a 45-minute session with you and [finance lead] on [date]. If that slips, what would be in the way?" [practitioner]

## Ending a demo

- Hold the demo until the buyer has stated an explicit need; features shown first create objections and price concerns (SPIN); see `references/discovery.md`, section Demo request before discovery. [data]
- Default: demo to the champion alone before the group, so one advocate sits in the room rather than two evaluators. Exception: a small-ticket tool, demo fast and ask. [practitioner]
- End with the buyer's own summary of what it solves, then the advance; seller feature recaps are the feature-dumping that cut win rates from 26% to 5% (Gong 2021, 8,382 deals). [data]
- Name the remaining stakeholders and the process before leaving: "who else has to see this, and what happens on your side between now and a signature?" [practitioner]
- Tell the buyer how this usually goes from here and what to avoid; they are buying for the first time and you have seen the path many times. [practitioner]

## Proposal sent, now what

- Send the proposal only after a hypothetical yes: "if you saw a solution that did this, in this time, for this money, would you move forward?" Wait for an emphatic yes. [practitioner]
- Co-author the scope with the buyer before the final version so the document reads as built for them; a proposal they helped write needs fewer rounds. [practitioner]
- Default: three genuinely different tiers, middle recommended; narrow to one recommendation plus one alternative for an indecisive buyer or a fixed-scope RFP (`references/offer-positioning.md`, section Building a three-tier proposal). [study]
- Put the buyer's next action and date in the cover note, not "let me know your thoughts"; an open-ended ask is a continuation, not an advance (SPIN). [data]
Written follow-up, at most 75 words: "Attached is the proposal we scoped on Tuesday: option B is my recommendation; the others are there for comparison. You said finance needs it by the 14th. I will hold the 16th at 10 for the review with you and [name]; if that date does not survive, tell me what changed and I will adjust." [practitioner]
- Silence after a proposal is usually indecision, not rejection: run a ping ("at this stage most buyers worry about X; is that where you are?") before any discount or deadline (JOLT). [data]
- Reopened concerns after an agreed vision are normal; buyers relitigate between intent and action. Answer each in full on the call rather than deferring it to the next one (JOLT). [data]

## Asking for the order in a single-call sale

These are (small-ticket, single-call) tactics. Each costs trust in the direct test (Hawes 1996), so stop at the first clear no in any relationship sale.

- Ask for the order only after four yeses: will this get you what you want, do you trust us to deliver, will it work for you, can you access the money. (small-ticket, single-call) [practitioner]
- When hesitation shows, ask "I'm sensing some hesitation; what are your main concerns?" and resolve the stated concern before asking again. (small-ticket, single-call) [practitioner]
- Default: resolve the concern and ask once more. Exception: in a petty-cash, single-call sale, loop while the buyer stays engaged; repeated closing cut success beyond that (SPIN/Huthwaite). [data]
- Play out the fear: "what are you afraid of happening? Let's walk through the worst case and the best case" turns a vague stall into two concrete scenarios. (small-ticket, single-call) [practitioner]
- Mistake: capturing the card "as standard procedure" or a delayed charge the buyer can cancel; these are low-ball moves. In any repeat-business setting state the price and ask plainly. [practitioner]
- Mistake: forceful or choice-restricting wording ("you have to", "no other option makes sense") → "it's your call; here's what I'd pick and why"; restriction triggers reactance in low-involvement buyers (Fransen 2015). [study]
- Stacked yeses before the ask raise verbal agreement, not purchase behaviour; count the sale when payment clears (Feeley 2012). [study]

## Mutual action plan

- Evidence is vendor-only and correlational: Outreach reports 26% higher win rates with a shared plan; Trumpet reports 29% rising to nearly 60%. No independent study exists. [data]
- Keep the plan to 6–20 completed steps; plans over 30 steps performed like no plan at all (Trumpet, vendor data). [data]
- Write each step as an if-then owned by a named buyer: who does what, by when, and what happens if blocked (Sheeran 2024, 642 tests: d = .27–.66, larger when specific and rehearsed). [study]
- Build the plan backwards from the buyer's go-live date and include the buyer-side gates (security review, legal, budget approval) the seller cannot see. [practitioner]
- Rehearse the plan aloud with the champion: rehearsed if-then plans produced the larger effects (Sheeran 2024). [study]
- Use the plan as a safety net, not a control device: a first-six-months roadmap with stage gates, owners and metrics is what reduced fear of failure (JOLT). [practitioner]

## Procurement and multi-stakeholder sign-off

- Negotiate the process before the substance: ask early how long a deal like this takes, who has to sign, and what speeds or slows it; otherwise late concessions meet "let me ask my boss". [practitioner]
- Process script: "How long does it usually take your organisation to do a deal like this? Who all needs to sign on? What might speed it up, and what might slow it down?" [practitioner]
- Bid only when you shaped the evaluation criteria or have a champion inside; an RFP you first see at release is usually written around a rival, so decline it or qualify hard before investing. [practitioner]
- Reopen criteria that exclude you by asking the champion what each requirement is meant to achieve, then propose outcome wording; criteria move before scoring starts, rarely after. [practitioner]
- Normalise the bumps: tell the champion which delays are typical (security, legal redlines) and the path through them, so a setback does not read as a dying deal. [practitioner]
- Do procurement's work for them: ask for their forms and fill them in; state precisely what you do and do not do, or they classify you as high risk and send the heavy contract. [practitioner]
- Ask how long IT or security diligence takes; if it is backed up, split the paper into a services contract that starts now and a technology contract that waits. [practitioner]
- Know the signer (CFO, legal, business head) before procurement ends and send them a few lines on what they are signing; a "what is this?" query sends you to the back of the queue. [practitioner]
- Count the deal when the purchase order exists, not at verbal approval; start no work before signature. [practitioner]
- Help the champion write their victory speech: ask how they will explain this choice to their boss or board, then give them that paragraph. [practitioner]

## Negotiating price and terms

### First offers and anchors

- Default: make the first offer, with a reason. Exception: when the buyer knows the market better than you, or you cannot tell what they know, let them open (Maaravi & Levy 2017, role-play). [study]
- When you know the market and the buyer does not, their opener can land above yours; ask "what budget have you set aside?" before quoting (Maaravi & Levy 2017, d = 0.96 in role-play). [study]
- Open with a precise, justified number rather than a round one: precise first offers drew more conciliatory counteroffers (Mason et al. 2013). [study]
- Expect moving first to pay (g = 0.42) and ambitious openers to raise deal value (g = 1.14), with more impasses (g = −0.42) and resentment (g = −0.40) (Petrowsky 2025 meta-analysis). [study]
- Discount these effects in enterprise deals: they shrink as issues multiply, and 366 of 374 effects came from lab or vignette tasks (Petrowsky 2025, 90 studies, N = 16,334). [study]
- Anchor with a reasoned number (value estimate, comparable deal, cost basis): anchors that looked random had no effect across 393 effects (Röseler & Schütz 2022 preprint, N = 17,708). [study]
- Keep the opener inside the zone of possible agreement: anger at a high opener, not the number itself, drove impasse and lower satisfaction (Petrowsky 2025 experiments, N = 2,121). [study]
- Learn the buyer's priorities before anchoring on a multi-issue bundle: an opener that reveals which issues you value invites a feigned conflict to extract concessions (Petrowsky 2025; Loschelder 2016). [study]
- Counter an anchor you receive by naming your target and their alternatives aloud; focusing on those neutralised the first-mover advantage (Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001). [study]
- Price as a stated fraction of proven value where you have the numbers: "we save you X a year; we are asking for a tenth of it." [practitioner]

### Concessions

- Mistake: accepting a first offer on the spot. Pause, test it, then accept: instant acceptance lowered the offerer's satisfaction even with equal or better outcomes (Galinsky et al. 2002). [study]
- Trade every concession for something: a discount buys a longer term, earlier cash, more volume or a committed start date; a concession given free becomes the new starting point. [practitioner]
- Hold one price for every customer; move scope or terms instead (a de-featured version, payment timing, term, volume), since a quiet exception becomes the floor. [practitioner]
- Publish the levers before the goal-line: price moves only along volume, payment timing, commitment length and deal timing, so a late-arriving executive already knows the structure. [practitioner]
- Concede slowly in shrinking steps and label each one ("this is the last move on term; it is not available on price") so it reads as wise rather than desperate. [practitioner]
- Mistake: answering a proposal with an instant counterproposal. Test and summarise theirs first: skilled negotiators made 1.7 immediate counterproposals per hour vs 3.1 (Huthwaite, 49 skilled negotiators, 103 sessions). [data]
- Give one strong reason for your price, not five; each extra reason exposes a weak point (the one-reason rule and its evidence: `references/objections.md`). [data]
- Replace self-labels such as "fair" and "generous offer" with the reason itself: they persuade nobody and irritate; skilled negotiators used 2.3 irritators per hour vs 10.8 (Huthwaite). [data]
- Signal a ridiculous offer immediately, in the room; a rejection sent days later is not credible. Decide the signals before the meeting. [practitioner]

### Buyer states a position ("we need 15% off") or threatens to walk

- Ask what sits behind a position before answering it: "what would 15% off let you do internally?" often reveals an approval threshold that a term or phasing change solves. [practitioner]
- Offer three packages of equal value to you and ask which they like best; the answer reveals the trades they value. [practitioner]
- Walk while keeping the door open: "it is just not economically rational for me at that number"; a buyer's walk-away costs them nothing, so restate the trade rather than chase. [practitioner]

### Voss labels and calibrated questions

No sales trials exist for these; use them as wording, not as levers.

- Label the concern, not the emotion, and wait for "that's right"; labelling and calibrated-question wording lives in `references/objections.md`, section Handling price pushback and discount requests. [practitioner]
- Run an accusation audit before a hard conversation: list what they could hold against you ("you probably feel we came in high") so it loses its charge. [practitioner]

### Buyer pushes back on price

Buyer: "that is almost double what we pay today."
- Seller: "That's a fair reaction. Help me understand what the current spend covers, and which of the four levers, volume, term, payment timing or start date, you'd move first." [practitioner]
- If pressed for a flat discount: "Every comparable customer pays this; I will not price you differently from them. If a longer term or earlier payment works, the model gives you something back." [practitioner]

## Next-step phrasing template

Implementation-intention format (Sheeran 2024, 642 tests: d = .27–.66, strongest when specific, contingent and rehearsed). The buyer says it back; at most 40 words.

> "[Buyer] will [action] by [date]. If [likely blocker], then [buyer] will [fallback] and tell [seller] by [date]. [Seller] will [seller action] by [date]."

- Fill every bracket with a name and a date before the call ends; "we'll circle back" has no if-then and is a continuation (SPIN). [data]
- Have the buyer say the plan in their words and repeat it in the confirmation email; stating and rehearsing the plan is what produced the larger effects (Sheeran 2024). [study]
- Keep the "if blocked" clause: the contingent if-then format outperformed plain intentions (Sheeran 2024). [study]

## Worked examples

### Ending a discovery call with a next step

Buyer: "This is useful, send me something and I'll take a look."
Seller: "Happy to. Does the decision sit with you and Priya in finance?"
Buyer: "Yes, she'd need to see numbers."
Seller: "Then the useful next step is a 45-minute session with you both where we build the cost-of-waiting case from your figures. Thursday at 10 or Friday at 2?"
Buyer: "Thursday."
Seller: "Will you get Priya's acceptance by tomorrow? If she can't make Thursday, what's the fallback?"
Buyer: "I'll ask today. If not, the following Tuesday."
Seller: "I'll send the invite and a one-page agenda within the hour."
Why it works: an advance, buyer-owned, dated, with an if-then and a self-prediction, confirmed in writing.

### Deeply indecisive buyer after the proposal

Buyer: "We love it, but I want to look at two more vendors and run it past the wider team."
Seller: "Most buyers at this point worry less about which vendor and more about being the name on the contract if rollout slips. Is that closer to it?"
Buyer: "Honestly, yes."
Seller: "My recommendation: finance module only, one-year term at the three-year price. Our onboarding lead maps the first ninety days with you, owners and gates included. Miss month two's gate and you pause without penalty. Does that make the decision smaller?"
Why it works: judge, recommend, limit, de-risk; no urgency added.

### Discount request on a $60k services proposal

Buyer: "We need 15% off to get this approved."
Seller (pauses): "Understood. What does 15% unlock on your side: a budget line, or an approval threshold?"
Buyer: "Anything over $55k goes to the CFO."
Seller: "So the target is $55k this year, not 15%. Two options, equal for us: phase two starts in Q1, invoiced then, so this year costs $52k; or full scope at $60k and I join the CFO call. Which works?"
Buyer: "The phased one."
Seller: "Then the phase-two start date goes into the agreement now."
Why it works: interest behind the position, package not discount, concession traded for a dated commitment.

## Common mistakes and replacements

| Mistake | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| Ending the call with "I'll send some info" | Propose the highest realistic advance, with the buyer's name and a date on it (SPIN; Gong 2018) [data] |
| Counting a verbal "yes, let's do it" as a win | Count the signed order or purchase order; verbal compliance outruns behaviour (Feeley 2012) [study] |
| Opening with an extreme anchor to "leave room" | Open ambitious but reasoned and inside the zone of agreement (Petrowsky 2025; Röseler & Schütz 2022) [study] |
| Giving a discount for nothing | Trade it for term, cash timing, volume or a start date, and label the trade [practitioner] |
| Instant counterproposal to the buyer's number | Test and summarise their proposal first (Huthwaite) [data] |
| Bidding on an RFP you first saw at release | Bid only where you shaped the criteria or have a champion; otherwise decline or qualify hard [practitioner] |
| Running the single-call script in a committee deal | Pick the motion row first; pressure tolerance and who owns the next step differ [practitioner] |
| A mutual action plan of 30+ steps used as a control tool | 6–20 buyer-owned if-then steps used as a safety net (vendor data; Sheeran 2024) [data] |
